Eki’s band sent Argi and Gaizka off with warm touches and a gift of dried meat. They continued toward the Evening. Argi pictured that land. It must always be without Sun and ruled by lynx and wolf and small creatures. She would not travel far enough to discover the truth, but she did not care. Argi was sent to Witness, and she would Witness the domain of the Sun, its people, the lives and deaths within. She did not wish to visit the land of the Evening.

Argi smiled when she thought of her River Cousins. Gaizka, who always knows what to do, loved them as well. He had used his wit and his strength to bring joy to the Cousins. Argi realized that she had also brought them joy. Her Witness, shared with them, was a sweet thing. Nerea talked often about Argi’s children and her children’s children and how for a hundred turnings of the seasons people would share their Witness. Argi never expected to feel such appreciation in this life. It filled her with joy, thinking about Eki’s kin shouting his name. She understood a truth then, perhaps the beginning of an answer to Nerea’s question. In sharing her own Witness, the Witnesses of others had been changed, molded, made better.

Argi and Gaizka walked through the valley between the mountains. When the Sun began to fall, they made camp. The pair found a rock overhang in a limestone gorge, close enough to the river to hear its music. Argi started the fire and Gaizka gathered twigs to feed it. They laid bracken fern on the stone floor so that they would be warm through the night. As they sat down to enjoy the gifts of Eki’s kin, Argi saw that Gaizka was troubled.

“Is it too cold for you, Gaizka? We can gather more wood to feed the fire.”

“Thank you, Argi, but the fire is warm.”

“Then what is wrong?”

Gaizka chewed the dried meat slowly, staring off towards the river.

“You brought Eki’s kin great joy in your Witness.”

“Yes,” Argi smiled. “Thank you.”

“On some nights, I wish that I had been born like you. I would have become a Witness, like you, and brought joy to all of our River Cousins.”

Argi considered this. Nerea had spoken of Witnesses born of the Moon, but it was rare. She knew she was blessed to be a Witness and to bring joy to those like Eki’s kin. But without Gaizka, what would she have Witnessed that day?

“Gaizka, who fashioned the ropes for brave little Eki? Who brought the little one honey and dried his eyes? This was you. When you hunt and when you return to our kin with ibex or boar, do they not smile with joy? This is your gift, Gaizka.”

“They do smile, but only for a short while. By the time the Sun turns, the meat and their smiles have gone. People will visit your Witness for many generations. Even when you have returned to the soil, people will see your Witness and smile with memory.”

“But, Gaizka, I could not do what you do! If I were on this journey alone, I would surely starve.”

“I think you are wrong. Hunting is not too difficult. I learned easily. Be patient. Find your quarry. Slay them quickly.”

Argi nodded. She listened to the music of the river and the fire.

“Teach me, then,” she said.

Gaizka laughed, but Argi only looked at him. He raised his eyebrows and swallowed his meat.

“Now?”

“We will sit in the brush before the river and wait for a great auroch to come for water.”

Gaizka’s brow furrowed, and he narrowed his eyes. Then, he stood up, smiling.

“Not an auroch, Argi, not without our kin to help,” He pulled his sister to her feet. “And your quarry will smell us in the brush. The wind will carry our scent to them. We will cross the river and climb the hill on the other side.”

The setting Sun cast long shadows through their camp as Gaizka strode away.

“Bring my hunting pouch!” he shouted.

Argi giggled, grabbed the pouch with its arrows and blade and twine, and hurried after her brother.

The red Sun had just vanished behind the mountains by the time the pair found their perch. They looked over the river, its surrounding mud and stone and brush. The river was narrow here, barely too wide to leap across. Argi leaned against a hard-leaved evergreen which had burrowed powerfully into the limestone and tightened her grip on Gaizka’s bow.

“Do you remember how to use the bow?” asked Gaizka.

“Yes. Of course,” answered Argi. “But I am afraid I will waste your arrow if I miss.”

“You will not miss. Use the tree to steady yourself.” Gaizka acted out his command. “We will wait for a hare or roe deer. If you strike with skill, they will not get far before giving up their spirit.”

Argi grunted, and the two waited. A chill began to settle over the darkening hills. Argi watched as stars began to dance upon the river’s surface. Entranced, she almost didn’t hear Gaizka’s hushed urgings.

“Do you see her?” Gaizka whispered sharply. He stretched out his arm, and Argi followed his finger with her eyes.

There it was. A roe deer. Argi wished it were a hare, but she did not show her uncertainty to Gaizka. The deer approached the water slowly. Her ears twitched, and Argi saw her nose working, searching for danger.

“Calm, Argi. Breathe slowly. Wait for her to show you her side, then aim just behind her front leg. Your arrow will strike her heart and lungs and kill quickly.”

Gaizka removed his hand from Argi’s shoulder and slid carefully behind her. He did not speak anymore.

The roe deer drew closer to the water, but she did not show Argi her side. Her face was pointed in Argi’s direction. She bent towards the river, taking first a quick lap, then a deep drink. Argi grew restless. She felt that the deer knew where she was, purposefully hiding itself from her. In her perch, she could not move for a better shot.

A branch broke in the brush behind the deer. The deer jumped and whirled away from the noise. Argi, afraid of losing her quarry, loosed her arrow. It struck the deer’s stomach as the animal turned. The deer let out a terrible cry, but it was barely slowed by Argi’s arrow. It vanished in the brush. Its cry echoed in Argi’s ears.

Anguish washed over Argi as she turned to Gaizka. His face was solemn.

“The arrow struck her belly. She will die, but it will take time and she will be in great pain.”

“Maybe a lynx will find her and end her suffering.” Argi pleaded.

Gaizka nodded slowly. “Maybe. But she is our responsibility. Do not be saddened, Argi. Even the very best hunters miss their arrows. Follow close behind me, and be very quiet.”

Gaizka crept carefully down from their perch with Argi close behind. They came to the river and crossed it. Argi watched Gaizka inspect the brush and the roe deer’s blood spattered about. As he made his way deeper beyond the brush, Argi marveled at her brother. He moved with determination and purpose. His eyes flitted this way and that, searching for the deer’s path. Argi could not see well in the dark woods, but Gaizka never slowed.

They reached a rocky, wooded hill and began to climb. A beam of moonlight broke through the forest’s canopy, and Argi saw a glistening dribble of something dark on a large stone.

“Gaizka! There!” she whispered.

“Yes. She is close. You must move exactly as I do.”

Argi obeyed. She followed in Gaizka’s footprints as they climbed higher. Cresting the hill, Gaizka held up a hand. Argi froze and followed his eyes.

The roe deer lay alone on a bed of flattened grass. Argi thought that this must be the deer’s home. She could not see the wound, but the animal’s breaths came quickly and uneven. Argi felt as though she shared her pain. She hoped that Gaizka would slay his quarry quickly, and she knew that he would.

Gaizka moved slowly, gracefully, like a heron creeping through a riverbed. He drew an arrow from his pouch, notched it in place, and pulled his bow string taut. Argi knew suddenly that she was Witnessing a great treasure and that she must share this Witness. Gaizka released the arrow. A moment passed where Argi heard nothing but the blade cutting through air. With much less noise than Argi had expected, the arrow struck the roe deer just behind her front haunch. She tried to stand but could not. She spasmed, and her breaths grew huge and heaving. Then, the deer’s spirit left her.

Argi breathed for the first time in a long while. Gaizka moved immediately towards his quarry. He drew his blade but, after groping around the deer’s neck, decided he did not need it. It was strange to see her brother so close to the creature. She had been small, smaller than Gaizka. Before Argi had even the chance to praise his skill, Gaizka had tied the deer’s legs with twine and heaved her onto his back.

“Let us go back to our camp now.”

They were not far from their fire. It seemed the roe deer had also judged this area to be safe and dry. Gaizka laid the deer carefully on the stone near the fire and drew his blade.

“I will clean her,” said Argi. “This much I can do.”

Gaizka smiled at his sister.

“Very well.”

He handed the blade to Argi, and she began to clean the animal. She cut the hide free first in two large pieces. She removed the deer’s damaged gut and intestines. She found the liver, removing it carefully, and brought it to Gaizka.

“Your quarry, brother.”

“No, Argi. This is still your quarry. I only helped.” He tried handing the prize back to Argi.

“It seems I was right,” said Argi. “I cannot do what you do. I do not have the patience or skill. I will not eat of the liver or of the heart. Death must be brought quickly, and I failed.”

“Do not be so proud, Argi. We will both eat of the liver and of the heart. This was our quarry.”

“I did not help you. I brought terrible pain to the roe deer.”

“Witness her. Mark this rock with her memory. Then, you will have done what I cannot. Her spirit will be known and we may both eat of the prize.”

Argi closed her eyes and nodded.

“This is right,” she said. She opened her eyes and they were filled with mischief.

“But you must help me.”

Gaizka tried to resist, but Argi would not listen. She set him on painting himself, poised in a crouch. She Witnessed the roe deer with reverence, marking the wall with her form and her blood and her ragged breaths. When she was finished, she looked upon her brother’s Witness with a frown.

“Gaizka, it seems we were both right. You cannot do what I do.”

Gaizka looked up from his Witness, angry for a moment, but bursting into a great laugh when he saw Argi’s mocking face. She laughed too, and bettered his depiction with a few sweeps of her brush. They looked upon their Witness as they shared the deer’s liver and heart, savoring the prize. She wondered what Nerea would think. A Witness hunting roe deer. A Hunter Witnessing to the mountain. She decided that this story would be for only her and her brother and those who came upon their Witness.
