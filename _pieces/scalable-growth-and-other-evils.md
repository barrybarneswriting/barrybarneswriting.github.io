I was four beers and a bong hit deep into my fifth hour of Fox News when Mike Lindell spoke to me. Don’t freak out. I was hate-watching. Like when you can’t look away from roadkill, or how my little brother spends so much of his free time watching those recordings of violent retribution being visited upon Mexicans who’ve crossed the Cartel in one way or another. I can’t stand those videos. In fact, I worry for my little brother.

But it was 1 a.m., I had a real buzz going, and Mike Lindell was selling Freedom Pillows. His mustache flailed as he spoke of his illustrious clients. He proudly hailed his Pillows as “Made in America” (by Americans? the scrupulous viewer may have asked). As far as I could tell, the Freedom Pillows had been dubbed so on account of the 2-by-2 American flag printed on the label. Call 1-800-LINDELL for 15% off your first order, he crooned.

Mike Lindell, permanently damp in that way common to twice-divorced Baby Boomers, changed my life that night. I began thinking deeply about the Freedom Pillow and the apparent market that existed for such products. Further research revealed an entire universe of similar items. Freedom Beer (Veteran-owned and operated), Anti-Woke Coffee Mugs, toilet brushes shaped like Nancy Pelosi. The prices for such products were invariably higher than their woke counterparts. The companies swam in positive reviews.

I was overcome with a desire to join their ranks, to stand side-by-side with these Culture Warriors and to snatch a piece of the pie for myself. First, I needed a product. Anybody who’s anybody can sell a Custom Ink t-shirt. That market was oversaturated and out of reach. The Traditional Values Laptop could play, but I feared high input costs and insurmountable barriers to entry. I turned a dozen ideas over in my substance-addled mind and came to no conclusion.

I decided to sit upside down on my couch (legs dangling over the back) to increase blood flow to my brain. As I assumed the position, a headline on the television caught my eye. It read, “Poll: 64% of School Principals LGBT or Marxist”. I felt my face swell as gravity did its job. Sweet lucidity coursed through me in a rush. Gay communist teachers. There it was. The nation’s boogeyman was to become my cash cow.

A familial contact had previously made me aware of an ecosystem of websites wherein educators buy and sell worksheets and other academic resources. This would be my beachhead, my Normandy, from which all other victories would follow. My targets would be homeschooled students between the ages of five and ten. Keep it simple. My weapon would be generative AI. Within a couple of hours, I had a library of content covering history, English, and science. The company would be called Right Path Education (Early education is tough, especially when you’re doing it on your own. Set your children on the Right Path today).

My ChatBot assistant wrote a moving 250 words for the company’s “About Us” section on our commerce platform. Good-natured and genuine. Not too many big words. Write it again, but include more phrases like “Family Values” and “Protecting our Children”. Better. Add references to men like Ronald Reagan and other conservative heartthrobs. Try again, and remove all mention of Dean Cain. Please remove all mention of Jon Voight. Perfect!

Okay. I had the product, the target demographic, and the branding. I did not share Mr. Lindell’s Rolodex of movers and shakers, so I would be relying on the old-fashioned way of marketing: real-time personalized advertisement delivery. I opened my right-wing social media of choice and navigated to the “Launch Campaign” page. However excited I was by my fledgling enterprise, I knew that this was still an experiment. I invested just one hundred dollars. This number can be seen as a budget, with each person reached by my advertisements subtracting a fraction of a cent from said budget. I manipulated the range slider, briefly reminded of my Icarian Bumble stint, and selected users between the ages of 28 and 40. I launched my campaign and retired for the night.

Sunday afternoon rolled around something like two days later. My maneki-neko had been damaged. I knew not when. Her paw, previously waving a perpetual hello or goodbye, hung limply at her side. I stared at the ceramic and the ceramic stared back, impotent. It was during this time that I received an alert from the Right Path’s social media informing me that my \$100 had been spent and prompting me to “Explore \[my\] Insights”.

I clicked, and my screen became a swirl of purple and blue. A golden brick appeared, and I clicked again. The brick shattered, revealing my first piece of data: 16,799 Americans between the ages of 28 and 40 had seen advertisements for the Right Path. I clicked the arrow on the right side of the screen, anxious for more. If just a third of these viewers became customers, I could afford to have my maneki-neko repaired. I might even be able to buy her a companion.

The second data point informed me that my click-through rate, the percentage of viewers who actually clicked on the advertisements, was just over 6%. Something must have gone wrong, was my first thought. My second thought was that I had been scammed out of \$100. I pictured 15,000 people scrolling past my advertisement, totally apathetic to the growing scourge of bisexual educators. A final click revealed my conversion rate, how many clickers turned into buyers. This number sat a depressing, though symmetrical, 6%. With a commission of \$2 per download, I was in the green by about \$20. Regardless, the wind had gone out of my sails. The repairs to my lucky cat would have to wait.

I flopped over the couch, reflecting on my failure. Had I miscalculated? Perhaps there was no market for anti-woke academic resources. Perhaps conservative parents prioritized education and expertise over the AI garbage I had worked so hard on.

Further research was needed. I dove headfirst into a world of SEO, CPC, and AOV. I learned about upselling and downselling and cross-selling and dropshipping. I came to a fascinating conclusion. Nay, a revelation! One that could change my life forever. The world, even.

As it turns out, the industry average CTR for display advertisements regarding educational materials is 0.57%. I had beaten this number by an order of magnitude. Beyond that, my conversion rate was over double what your bog standard company expects. The humble six, oft overlooked. Six seems small when you consider the hundreds of numbers larger than it. But in my world, the wonderful world of ecommerce, six suddenly felt like my best friend.

Three months passed. I left six rotting in the hole from whence he came. I had a new love, and she had curves in all the right places. It took a few iterations of my marketing practices, but I pinpointed a strategic error. On Twitter, I was shooting fish in a barrel. I could have been spewing Agent Orange across entire fish villages. Gab, Rumble, 8Chan. I braved the dregs of the internet and was rewarded with a new CTR: the ever lovely 8%.

This was around when the real money started coming in. I hired a Japanese man working at Home Depot (I know, right?) to restore my maneki-neko to her original state. I quit my job. Revenue from the Right Path couldn’t completely replace my salary, but I didn’t care. I was making money more efficiently. My ChatBot was now programmed to produce single sentence summaries of essential business books once a week. Your thoughts become your reality. Outsource your labor to the Third World.

Despite Tim Ferriss’ words echoing in my ears, I did not outsource my labor. I found a strange sort of love for it. It wasn’t the product itself. I did my best not to think about what I was putting out into the world. There was no art nor science to it. It was the advertising that had stolen my heart.

See, internet broadcasters had become my superhighway to success. The smarmy liberals of network television tend to unfairly flatten a deeply textured landscape of right-wing thought leaders into a sort of matte beige smear. The front line guys are the toxically masculine, red meat-loving flexers. My analytics told me that the audience for these types tends to skew younger, not yet old enough to purchase or recommend my worksheets. I had to dig deeper if I was to unearth the acute form of high-functioning mental illness I was looking for. Fursuit-wearing Nazi VTubers, chalkboard-ranting oldtimers, unlicensed rebroadcastings of Little House on the Prairie. Down went my yuri-ita into these streams and up came thousands of customer-shaped golden nuggets.

Within another three months, I knew it was time to go legit. My humble ecommerce brand became a limited liability corporation. I was now the CEO and sole proprietor of The Right Path Education, LLC. I had tried to have my ChatBot added to the executive suite, but, according to the Internal Revenue Service, robots “lack legal standing”. No matter. My once ordinary studio apartment had also been transformed into a state-of-the-art educational dojo. I replaced my couch with an ergonomically designed hanging egg chair. A Newton’s cradle now sat to the left of my computer monitor and a miniature Zen garden sat to the left of that. Even my maneki-neko had a new friend. He was called Jin Chan, every business owner’s favorite capitalist toad.

Feedback came in droves. Direct messages, social media shoutouts, and reviews on our website all screamed that the Right Path had freed them and their children from liberal society once and for all. This was, in general, below my pay grade. My ChatBot responded automatically with humor and warmth to any and all sentiment directed our way.

But a letter arrived one day. It only ended up in my hands because it was the Christmas season, and I enjoyed perusing the endless holiday sales. The envelope was plain with the address written neatly in a tight script. A stamp of Theodore Roosevelt ornamented the top right corner. Spurred on by the sheer novelty of a letter addressed to me, I gently tore the envelope open. Dan and Molly Whitaker were writing to thank me all the way from Appalachia. It was hard to find educational material with “good values”, they said, but my work had been a godsend. Their children had also attached notes. Thomas was seven and Maya was six. They called me Teacher. They wrote about how smart they were becoming and how I had been a big help to Daddy and Mommy.

I was moved by the notes, both the parents’ and the children’s. By the former, I was filled with disgust. Dan (I assume it was Dan) misspelled propaganda and used affect where he should’ve used effect. I was reminded why this was normally the ChatBot’s duty. The latter stirred something else within me. I saw the children, their messy brown hair, their sticky fingers, how hard they worked at my exercises. I was reminded of myself. Very briefly, I joined them in their lessons. I offered rapt attention to my conservative father. Briefer still, I recalled a night full of shouting and tears. Stinging at my temple. Something worse in my fragile, adolescent soul.

I felt my brows squeeze together and my grip tighten on the yellow notebook paper. More quickly even than the memories of a night purposefully forgotten, came an outrageous notion. Like a single frame of a film that had been switched out, I thought about giving it all up. Thomas and Maya. Jesus Christ.

From across the room, my computer sounded a lazy “DINNGGG”.

A cooperative from Florida’s panhandle had completed a full curriculum purchase. That was a one-time spend of ten grand, with the opportunity to renew each year for half of that. The tension in my face vanished. I looked over at my Jin Chan, who was looking at my maneki-neko, who was waving to me. I smiled and waved back.

I dropped the envelope in my wastepaper basket and leaped into my hanging egg chair. Switching on the television, I let that familiar mustache drench the walls of my apartment with patriotic bravado. My fingers drifted to the bare skin below my nose. Perhaps it was time for one more change.
