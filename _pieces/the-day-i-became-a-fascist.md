It was 9:30PM when we arrived at Union Station. I already knew it was gonna be a shitshow. Union Station, really any major city’s train station, can be a shitshow at night, but Union Station holds a special place in my heart as the only one where I have feared for my girlfriend’s safety. My safety, too, if I’m being totally honest.

We get in at 9:30PM, and the Station is relatively barren. We have an hour and a half until our connecting train. That’s worst case scenario. It’s basically me, my girlfriend, one or two disinterested Amtrak employees, and people who have nowhere to go but this big, temperature-controlled building chock full of captive audience members.

As people often do after six hours on a bus, we zeroed in on the nearest fast food stall and got in line to order. There was a man, disheveled, mid-forties, running circles around the trashcan, talking to himself frantically. Fine. He was gonna do his thing, and all of us in line were gonna avoid eye contact and hope that whatever mental episode he was experiencing didn’t escalate any further. I glanced around and caught a lone young woman assessing the situation. She was clearly also on the hunt for a shitty chicken sandwich before her next train but decided to return to her seat.

There’s one guy ahead of us in line when I hear crinkling behind me. It sort of sounds like a bunch of trash bags. I turn around and have my suspicions confirmed in a way I hadn’t anticipated. The man behind me is wearing trash bags. Three of them, as far as I can tell. One black bag is tied to form a makeshift skirt. Another has holes cut out for his arms and neck, and the third adorns his head like a gigantic beanie. Christ.

He wasn’t doing anything out of the ordinary, aside from the trash bag clothing. Nonetheless, because I love my girlfriend and would prefer her not to have to stand within boner distance of what was damn close to a naked man in the train station, I stepped behind her, closer to the trash bag man. He never said anything rude or weird, never raised his voice. After our order, it was his turn, and if I were wearing a blindfold I would have thought nothing of the transaction.

During our time visiting this fast food stall, there is a woman pacing around all the while. I hear the squeaking of her two-tiered shopping cart’s wheels before I see it. I know she is going to ask for money or food as soon as she starts pushing it towards us, and I have a firm “No, sorry” response locked and loaded. Listen, I don’t like it either, but I’ve landed myself in too many sticky situations by engaging in the past. My girlfriend was there for several of them. As is probably apparent, I am already feeling somewhat on edge, so, yes, I turn down the woman before she has more than a few words out.

The woman with the shopping cart didn’t like that. I suppose I wouldn’t have either, if I were her. I’d turned pointedly away after my “No, sorry” but I heard a garbled exclamation leave her throat. She rolled slowly away, rocking a sort of whisper scream about how she just wanted something to eat. Why couldn’t anybody just fucking get her something to eat? I know, I know, but I also know that you’ve done exactly the same before.

Anyways, she rolls away but stays in the vicinity. Feeling her watchful eyes on us is unpleasant as we wait what feels like 20 minutes for our food. After an eternity, our number is called, we grab our order and our suitcases, and roll ourselves away. We pass the woman. I clench up ever so slightly, but nothing happens.

We made it to the ticket holders’ seating area. I was left with my thoughts for a moment as I ate the aforementioned chicken sandwich. As such, let’s take a moment to zoom out. Let’s break the fourth wall and discuss. If I were you, there is a decent chance I’d be thinking, “Wow, this guy’s a pussy”, and, fair enough. But I’m asking you to go with me here.

It has been a very long day. After six hours on a bus, I have arrived at the second busiest train station in the United States. I only highlight the mentally ill, homeless, or otherwise destitute people in the immediate vicinity of the fast food stall, but suffice to say there are many more. (I see a man dumping the contents of a trashcan on the ground and then absconding with said trashcan. I see somebody smoking… not weed… out of a glass pipe in the bathroom). These situations, while never inspiring anything close to abject terror, leave me unsettled. I am on edge, and I am angry at these people. The regular travelers outnumber them 10:1, yet we are all on their schedule. I feel like their wills are being imposed upon us. I feel like we are being punished or that we are at a disadvantage because we are following the rules, acting personably, etc. It adds significant mental load, and it pisses me off.

We waited the remaining hour and departed the ticket holders’ seating area. One familiar with Union Station may know there are many raised walkways winding throughout the concourse. Some connect to shops, others to the parking garage. As we approached the departure gates, I saw something that D.C. residents had probably gotten used to: a squad of national guardsmen (maybe four or five of them) were descending an escalator, surveying the space. I breathed a sigh of relief. They didn’t do all that much, just walked around in their military fatigues. We got in line for our train, and I watched them stroll up and down the concourse. Like magic, the assorted mentally ill, homeless, and otherwise destitute people that had been making my night that much more unpleasant demanded far less attention.

This is the promise that I am offered by fascism. I am a good citizen. I follow the rules and don’t speak too loudly or do drugs in public spaces. Yet, my life is full of moments where I am forced to defer to those who do. I and many others defer to these people out of the same basic notion: we have more to lose than they do. I could yell at the man smoking “not weed” in the bathroom, but what is to stop him from following me out the bathroom, breaking his pipe over my head, and facing the music? Are they going to throw him in jail? Something tells me that the man getting high on the public sink is not too fearful of such a fate.

This is where the National Guard come in, the explicit threat of violence. It is a remarkably effective strategy for making the “civilians” feel safe when surrounded by drug addicts or schizophrenics or half-naked men. The power dynamic fundamentally shifted when four soldiers wielding assault rifles walked through the concourse. Their mere presence allowed me to breathe a little easier. Before the soldiers descended that escalator, I was pretty sure no one was going to assault me or my girlfriend. In fact, I’d put my confidence level at something like 95% sure. But, suddenly, that level was 100%.

We stand in front of the sign that will signal our departure gate for about 15 minutes. I watch the National Guard wander the concourse. They pass the bathrooms, the trash bag wearing man, the store that sells snacks and Atomic Habits, me and my girlfriend, Raising Cane’s, and the woman with the shopping cart. They turn around when they reach McDonald’s and walk back the way they came. They ascend the escalator and are soon out of sight.

I heard the crinkling of the trash bags. I heard the squeak of the shopping cart’s wheels. I found myself swiveling my head, ever so slightly, just to feel like I was fully aware of my surroundings. If only the men with guns had stuck around, I wouldn’t have felt so unsafe.
