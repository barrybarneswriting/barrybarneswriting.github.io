Consider the following fantasy:

You are standing in the general boarding line at Union Station. You glance at your ticket, reviewing the information one more time as the line starts to move: Northeast Regional, train number 121, Coach Class, car number seven, row twelve, seat B. Your travel companion, maybe a romantic partner, was automatically assigned the seat next to your own because you were careful to book in advance. You descend the escalator, and a noble Amtrak employee delivers their announcement on repeat.

“Please refer to the numbers outside the doors to find your car. Please do not enter the train until you find the car your seat is located in. Thank you and enjoy your trip.”

You follow the employee’s directions carefully. You spot car number four first, and you walk down the platform until you see the large number seven printed outside the doors. You pull your luggage over the gap, walk down to row twelve, heave your suitcase into the overhead storage, and sit down next to your companion. Your partner wants to get some work done on their laptop, so they pull the window’s curtain to minimize glare. You turn on your favorite podcast, kick your footrest into action, and lean your head back, looking forward to another relaxing train ride.

After writing the paragraphs above, I had to flex all of my muscles at the same time to get rid of the almost painful erection they gave me. It’s a fantasy, as unadulterated and sensual as any other. It has never happened to me. It probably never will. But, if I haven’t rubbed you the wrong way with my boner joke, please enjoy as I spend the rest of this piece delineating the arguments for three very simple improvements to the Amtrak experience, rebutting potential counterarguments, and generally fantasizing about what a United States train system could look like if the world were not so cruel.

First, the tiniest bit of background, since I promised more fantasy. I’ve taken lots of trains. I’m probably not in the 99.9th percentile or anything, but, for the past three and a half years I’ve been without a car, I have used trains for any long-distance travel. I’d comfortably estimate thirty train rides a year, on average. I’m not some train enthusiast or connoisseur, but I feel that I’ve done the legwork necessary to officially pass judgment.

I’ve ridden Business Class twice. I rode the Acela once. All other travel has taken place in the Coach Class of either the Northeast Regional or Keystone Service train lines. The Northeast Regional line is far and away the busiest service, with its nine million passengers per year nearly tripling second place’s ridership. Make these three changes to the Northeast Regional train line, and vastly improve the experience of almost ten million riders every year.

Do you know the perks of purchasing a Business Class ticket? I do. You get a free non-alcoholic beverage. Fine. That’s a fun perk for shelling out to escape the Wild West of Coach Class. The other perks are footrests, a curtain to block out unwanted sunlight, and assigned seats. I don’t deny that the seats are technically larger, but I can honestly report that I could not perceive any difference in my own.

The first two are so easy, so NOTHING, that I am almost more angry about them than the assigned seats thing.

See footnote number one for my full explanation, but, suffice to say, curtains are not that fucking expensive.[^1] I’m not asking for velvet or silk or whatever it is that luxury curtains are crafted out of. They could tape together construction paper for all I care. Sunlight can be annoying, especially in those moments when your train is zipping through medium-dense forest, turning it into something reminiscent of a strobe. What a simple comfort it would be to slide a curtain over the offending window. It offends me that such a basic accommodation is reserved for the Business Class. It should offend you, too.

The other easy one is footrests. And, listen, I’m not dying for footrests or anything. It just fills me with righteous fury that footrests are the lone privilege of Business Class passengers. Footrests would be nice. No footnote math for this one, but if fucking Greyhound can manage it, I think Amtrak can too.

Now for the crown jewel, for the wettest of wet train dreams: assigned seating. This is without a doubt the biggest perk of purchasing a Business Class ticket, especially for passengers traveling in pairs, but, as I will explain, the change would benefit everybody. There is no mad dash at the train station once the screen finally announces your departure gate. There is no “You check the front. I’ll check the back. Text if you find an open section.” There is no long march down the train as solo-rider after solo-rider avoids your hateful gaze, hoping that nobody asks if someone is sitting in that seat they have not-so-subtly placed their backpack in. There is simply “Walk to your car, find your assigned seats, and sit down.”

Why do I say this would benefit everyone? Well, I’ve also been a solo-rider, and I too have searched long and hard for an open pair of seats. It’s nice to sit alone. I get it. But, when you and I and every other solo-rider on the train has complete freedom to wander its entire length, things get clogged up. Seated passengers get clipped by stray backpack straps. The fragile diplomacy that takes place every time two passengers traveling in opposite directions pass by each other becomes incredibly common, often victimizing even already seated passengers as I wedge half of my body into their space to allow that mother and child to squeeze by.

I love you too, solo-riders, and I want what’s best for you. Your stated preference may be to hunt for that empty seat. Your revealed preference may very well match up, but I believe that the practice hurts you. I know because I’ve lived it. Am I really happier walking the length of six train cars? Maybe I find an empty seat, and I claim it. What follows? Every stop, as new people board, I watch them pass in my peripheral vision, teeth clenched as I wonder whether one of these assholes is going to sit down next to me.

To enforce assigned seating would be to eliminate a litany of unnecessary negative experiences for solo-riders, riders in pairs, and probably even the Amtrak employees who seem to spend so much of their time begging riders to find a seat.

I assume I don’t actually have to spend much time convincing you that assigned seating is desirable. It is one of the few actual perks of Business Class, after all. If it is as desirable as the Business Class premium would suggest, why not make it universal? I would like to engage with the strongest arguments, but there do not seem to be any.

Is it too hard? Recall my earlier “if fucking Greyhound can handle it” argument. Well, fucking Amtrak can handle it! They do it! They do it in Business Class, but they also do it for all Acela passengers, regardless of Class. Buses and trains all over the world handle the Herculean task of assigning seats. This is the United States! The land of James Watson and Thomas Edison and Henry Ford. Just rip the concepts from some Europeans and call it your own!

Maybe it would create some horrific nightmare of dynamic pricing, with more desirable seats costing much more than those near the bathrooms. I am fantasizing, so I am tempted to say only that this would not be allowed in my perfect system. You could pick your seat online on a first-come, first-served basis. If you book late, you might sit next to the bathroom, and that would be that.

However, I actually believe that the most desirable version of this system would involve a surcharge to choose your own seat. I don’t know what it would be, maybe ten percent of your ticket price or a flat $4 fee like I’ve seen on long-distance buses. Everyone gets an assigned seat. If you buy two seats in a single purchase, they will be assigned next to each other if at all possible, but passengers only get to choose their own seat if they pay the surcharge. This is a simple sorting mechanism. I am not unreasonable, even in my fantasies, and I find a small surcharge to exclude those passengers totally apathetic to their seat’s location to be reasonable.

The problem of dynamic pricing does not come close to convincing me. I am left with the conclusion that skeptical readers probably answered with as soon as I posed the question: they restrict the luxury of assigned seating to Business Class because they want to incentivize riders to pay the associated premium.

Amtrak is a federally chartered, for-profit corporation. That just means that it was created by an act of Congress, but it is supposed to operate like a business and generally cover its own expenses. Amtrak has never turned a profit and relies heavily on government money, much to Congress’ chagrin.

I’m not going to try and explain exactly why the low-effort, high-impact changes I’ve listed above have not been made, but Occam’s Razor instructs me to assume it has something to do with money. Business Class generates outsized revenue for Amtrak, and Congress pressures Amtrak to generate more revenue and become less of a tax burden, so Amtrak doesn’t want to decrease incentives for a purchase that increases revenue. If Amtrak withholds these basic amenities because Congress is pressuring it to raise revenues, maybe we could fix it by eliminating that pressure. I believe I’m calling for the nationalization of Amtrak and the railroads it uses.

This is my manifesto. I openly declare that my ends can only be attained by the forcible redistribution of those benefits which are now reserved only for the Business Class. What then, you may ask, will happen to the Business Class? I do not know. I do not care. I ride Coach, and I want a FUCKING footrest.

[^1]: Go with me on some back-of-the-envelope math. A standard eight-car Northeast Regional train is 749.33 feet long and carries 486 passengers. If I found a Coach Class ticket for $30 at a time that wasn’t the wee hours of the morning, I’d feel like I had benefited from a small stroke of luck. Let’s say every ticket sold, and every ticket sold for $30. Amtrak would collect ~$14,600 in ticket fare. I found a two-panel set of 42-inch curtains on Amazon for $10. You’d need 215 of these bad boys to shroud the entire length of the train on both sides. Some math whiz out there might have already concluded that this would cost $2,150. The truly numerically gifted may have noticed that this is less than 15% of the fare costs collected on our theoretical trip. The part of me which can still recall my high school economics course is shrieking about operating costs and overhead and incidentals and other bullshit I’m not interested in right now. I understand that Amtrak does not simply add $14,600 to their coffers after our trip. I’m just saying, the cost is almost CERTAINLY not the reason.
