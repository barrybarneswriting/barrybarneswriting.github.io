Think of this as the back of the book, or maybe the flap inside the front cover. The author’s note.

I’ve been experimenting with and researching this little project for a minute. It is inspired by cave paintings from the Spanish Levante. The intimacy of these scenes and the incomprehensible amounts of time that have passed since their creation really compelled me. These people, with brains identical to ours, living in a profoundly different world. Even more than this, I was moved by the revelation of shared experiences across millennia: war, hunting, even the simple pleasure of a sweet treat (as addressed in the passage you are about to read). So, I thought I would write a story about a woman (some researchers have suggested that women may have been among the primary image-makers in prehistoric Europe) who went on a great journey, leaving record of what she saw on the walls of mountains. Each chapter is a sort of hypothesis posed about a specific Levantine painting.

This is historical fiction, heavy on the fiction. There was no written language \~6,000 years ago. I am not even an archeologist or anthropologist, though I’ve leaned heavily on the actual experts in my best attempt to get as many details correctly as I can.

Our story is set in Mesolithic Spain, specifically the Iberian Peninsula. This was a precarious time for the Western Hunter Gatherers. Neolithic farmers were beginning to arrive from across the Mediterranean Sea. Within another millennium, the WHGs would be wiped from the genetic record (though, if it makes you feel better, the scientists seem to think this was a result of interbreeding and gradual absorption. No civilizational genocide stuff).

I will say nothing else, but I thought it may be helpful to the reader who is not me to have this bit of context. I hope you enjoy the very first installment of \"The Witness and the Hunter”.

* * *

From the River, the pair walked towards the Evening. Argi felt fearful in these early days. There were no fish in these growing hills, and she had never been so far from her kin. She learned to push this fear aside. She held Nerea’s Favor, and her Teacher would tell her to be brave. Gaizka held his bow and his arrows, and bravery came to him easily.

When they made their camp, her brother would sometimes disappear in the night, and she would wake to the smoke of a roasting hare. Argi set traps before they slept. Both collected the gifts of the soil as they walked. They ate well, and the skies were kind to them.

On the fifth night, they slept in the mountains. The sixth day was the first of climbing. Argi’s body was strong, but this was hard travel. She was rewarded in Witnessing a wise auroch, perched upon a great rock, look upon the valley below and fill it with his calls. Argi saw that Gaizka was frustrated. The auroch could have made his Quarry, but the distance was too far. Argi was sorry that Gaizka could not yet make his Quarry, but she felt her Teacher’s Favor and promised to share this Witness with her.

It took another passing of the Sun for the pair to hear the cries of a child. They walked in the valley between the mountains, and a young boy’s wail pierced the air. Gaizka looked to Argi. Argi nodded, and both hurried towards the noise.

A mother knelt with her child, the boy too young for words. An older boy, though younger than Argi and her brother, watched helplessly.

Argi spoke with concern.

“What has happened?”

The older boy was frightened.

“Ama!” he cried.

The boy’s mother turned to Argi, sharing her son’s fear for a moment. Her face brightened as she looked upon Argi.

“It is okay, Eki. These are River Cousins.”

The mother’s speech was not their own. The sounds came out differently, and some words Argi had never heard before. Still, the mother was right. They were River Cousins. Much about them was the same.

“Yes, Ama,” said Gaizka. “What has happened to the Child?”

She smiled as she stroked the Child’s hair.

“Nothing has happened to this one. He is tired of red deer. He is greedy and asks for honey.”

The older boy, Eki, spoke.

“I told Ama that there is honey in the cliffs, but she will not let me climb.”

“To climb the mountain for honey is a dangerous thing,” said Argi. “Your Ama needs you safe.”

Another wail from the child bounced through the valley.

Gaizka looked to Argi with wide eyes.

“Let us help you,” he said. He untied his sack and held up a coil of nettle rope. “We will tie you up so that you are safe. My Aita has used this many times when I look for honey in the tall branches of the holm oaks.”

The boy’s Ama frowned.

“Is this one to be trusted with Eki’s safety?” she asked Argi.

Gaizka smiled at Argi. She sighed and looked to the woman.

“He is trusted. I promise.”

Argi raised her Favor to the woman and pressed it to Gaizka’s forehead. The Ama nodded and sent the boy with his River Cousins.

Eki pointed to a small hole in the mountain wall. He led the way up a rocky channel, and the group came to be above their treasure.

“Do not fear,” said Gaizka. “I will tie you in the rope as my Aita taught me. You are small enough that Argi and I will not have trouble lowering you.”

“I do not fear,” said Eki.

Gaizka finished the knots. Argi set fire to a handful of rotten wood, let it burn for a moment, and blew out the flame. Thick smoke rose from the wood and spun into the wind.

“Hold this to the bees. They will be calmed and share their fruits with you more easily.”

Eki nodded and accepted the smoking branch.

The two began lowering the boy down the cliff. The ropes were strong, and the knots had been tied with great skill and attention. Eki shouted instructions up the cliff as he approached the bees’ nest.

The scent hit Argi first. That smell of rotten fruit, letting a wanderer know that they are not wanted here. Eki cried out, ropes jerking. Argi and her brother began to pull, but Eki shouted again.

“No, cousins! The bees were angry, but the smoke is calming them. Do not lift me up yet.”

The bees continued to sing, the sounds settling to a hum. Eki let out a few more pained yelps, but Argi and Gaizka did not pull him up. Argi thought of the boy’s Ama and hoped that he would not be stung too badly.

After a short time, Eki called out.

“I am finished. Pull me up.”

They pulled Eki over the cliff edge. He had been stung a handful of times. One finger was blown up like a slain ibex left too long. The right side of his face was swollen, his eye nearly disappearing behind red hot flesh. He smiled and let out a cry of victory.

He grasped Argi and Gaizka’s hands in thanks. The honey and brood clung to them with determination, and they cleaned themselves hungrily. Eki’s sack hung heavy with his treasure.

“Your Anaia will be quiet now, I am sure,” said Gaizka.

Eki and Argi murmured their agreement as they descended towards the boy’s family.

The woman ran to her son, pulling him close.

“My son, you are a fool!” she cried, though she betrayed her true feelings, kissing his swollen face and looking at the honey with pride.

“Let us bring your prize back to our kin and share it with them.”

Eki agreed. He allowed his Anaia to dip his hand into the honey, and Argi was reminded of how Gaizka used to cry for sweet figs. She watched him now. He was doing very well on this journey. To aid a River Cousin is a great thing, and she was lucky to Witness. She decided that this would be the first memory to record.

The family’s kin shared food with Argi and Gaizka. The woman’s Aita shared stories of a strange people from the sea, different from Cousins, with skin like dried grass in summer. Her Aita seemed to fear these people, with their pale skin and dark eyes, but many of his kin did not agree. Some said that other River Cousins had met these people and exchanged gifts of art and food.

Argi thought longingly of these people, wishing to Witness them. But she could not Witness to what she had not seen. With private embarrassment, she remembered that she had certainly seen something worth Witnessing.

Sneaking away, deeper into the rock, Argi prepared her tools. She pressed her own Favor to her lips and did the same with her Teacher’s. Out of her pouch, she set her ochre and fine brush of ibex fur on the stone floor. She prepared the wall, cleaning away dirt, the small creatures, and their debris. She held her two Favors and bowed towards the site which would carry her Witness. She found in her mind the memory and examined it. Eki hanging from the cliffside, so far from the ground. The rope Gaizka had fashioned. The bees buzzing around him with reluctant acceptance.

Argi painted. She Witnessed to the rock, filling it with memory. This mountain, as long as it stood, would tell the story of Eki. All those who found shelter in this cave would see Eki’s bravery, Gaizka’s ingenuity, and the gifts of the Earth, sweet enough to pacify a crying child.

Argi and Gaizka slept safely, surrounded by Eki’s kin. They woke to a gleeful shout. It was Eki. Somebody had discovered Argi’s Witness and had shared it with him. All his kin laughed and embraced him. Eki seemed to stand taller, with his chest puffed out like a proud brown bear. Argi asked herself Nerea’s most important question, though feeling no closer to an answer:

Why do we Witness?
