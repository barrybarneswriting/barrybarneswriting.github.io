So, it has been one week since the Oscars. One week since rising (risen?) star Timothée Chalamet and Marty Supreme went home empty-handed. I am going to discuss the Ballet or Opera Controversy (which I will refer to as the BOOC) in depth, but let’s get a few things out of the way.

First, I’m not making an argument about whether or not Timothée Chalamet or Marty Supreme should have taken home Oscars. I didn’t even see the movie. Second, I don’t think these comments likely had any effect on their losses that night. Oscar voting opened on February 26, two days after Variety published the conversation between Chalamet and Matthew McConaughey. According to this very scientific graph I have produced, the story as it is now understood, “Timothée Chalamet Disses Opera and Ballet!”, didn’t take off until March 5 at the earliest, the same day that Oscar voting closed.

It seems likely that Josh Safdie’s controversy, reported first on January 26th by Page Six, could have had significant impact, but, again, I’m not really interested in whether or not the movie or the actor should have brought home Oscars.

What I am interested in is the level of vitriol I have observed, both in-person and online, directed towards Chalamet in the days following that Variety video. Anecdotally, I spoke to a group of 20-somethings on the night of the Oscars who proclaimed that Chalamet had obliterated his chances for an award and that he deserved it because he was getting “high and mighty”. The list of celebrities that have blasted Chalamet following the BOOC include Whoopi Goldberg, Doja Cat, and Jamie Lee Curtis. Curtis stated that he shouldn’t have been “throw\[ing\] those art forms under the bus”. Doja Cat spent most of her TikTok describing how hard the people involved in opera and ballet productions work and pointed out that obviously these people and their audiences care. Whoopi Goldberg framed her short segment on Chalamet by stating that the actor “took some heat during his award season campaign for comments hyping up his talent” before characterizing his statement as “crap\[ping\] on the art form”. This is to say nothing about the torrent of Tweets, Threads, and Instagram posts that flooded the internet chastising Chalamet in the days preceding the Oscars ceremony.

This reaction feels off to me. Much of it feels dishonest. And I’m going to do my best to succinctly explain why.

Let’s dig in to the comments made by the three very famous people I identified above. Jamie Lee Curtis stated that her daughter dances and that Chalamet should not throw those art forms under the bus. So, did Chalamet throw these art forms under the bus? That is, did he criticize, blame, or harm these art forms in order to gain some advantage? Not even close, really.

What precisely were Chalamet’s comments, and what was the conversational context in which he made them? Well, the thread begins with Matthew McConaughey bemoaning modern audiences’ shrinking attention spans, faster-paced movies, and the death of true Act 1s. “Am I feeling something?” he asks Chalamet. “I think you are,” responds Timothée, before adding a layer of nuance. He points to a growing tension where, as more and more projects are pushed in the direction McConaughey identified, there is an increased demand for slower-paced cinema that takes its time to pull you in. He mentions an article he read which claimed that Gen Z is a bigger movie-going audience than millennials. Frankenstein, says Chalamet, is an example of a slower-paced movie that found great success at the box office.

Now, the moment of truth approaches. Background chatter muffles and an eerie hum radiates through the young actor’s town hall as he draws ever closer to the comments that will unleash a media circus upon him. “I’m really in the middle, Matthew”. Chalamet refers to the people who go on TV to say “Hey, we gotta keep movie theaters alive”, stating that he has both been the guy to do that and admires people who do so. The other side of this, as he sees it, is that people will go see movies that they want to see. Barbie and Oppenheimer, both massive successes, got people out of the house and loud and proud about going to see them. No one had to beg the audience.

For the fullest possible understanding to be shared between us, I have transcribed the next bit of the conversation below:

TC: And I don’t wanna be working in ballet or opera, or, you know, things where it’s like, hey, keep this thing alive. Even though it’s like —

MM: Yeah, yeah, yeah, yeah, yeah

TC: no one cares —

MM: Yeah, yeah, yeah

TC: about this anymore. All respect to the ballet and opera people out there. But, yeah. I just took shots for no reason.

MM: That’s not a shot.

TC: What a shame.

MM: I hear what you’re saying.

TC: Yeah! So… sorry.

These yeah-yeah-yeahs are always — and not necessarily wrongfully — left out of the various transcriptions of Timmy’s quote. Likewise, McConaughey’s “That’s not a shot” is nowhere to be found. “I hear what you’re saying”, says the Academy Award winner. This final comment is a charitable acknowledgement that, even if Timmy didn’t word it in the most delicate way, McConaughey understands the real point and truth that Timmy was getting at. Well, McConaughey, me too! And I don’t think its very difficult to understand.

The various criticisms leveled against Chalamet seem to be based on two basic contentions: 1. People do care about ballet and opera and 2. Chalamet is getting too big for his britches by shitting on art forms with many hundreds of more years of history than film.

People care about ballet and opera. Thousands of people produce, perform in, and attend such performances every year. But, of course, this isn’t what Chalamet was referring to. He is speaking of the “general audience”. It is not hard to see that he is, basically, correct. According to the National Endowment for the Arts’ Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (SPPA), opera ticket sales in the United States peaked some time around 1982 at 5.1 million attendees. Movie theater sales in the United States peaked in 2002, when American cinemas sold 1.85 billion tickets. In 2022, movie theaters sold around 700 million tickets; operas sold 1.8 million. Can you soberly assess these numbers and say that Chalamet was off base? I can’t. Neither can industry experts.

Let’s continue to treat Chalamet’s claims with good faith. He used opera and ballet to draw a contrast between less popular art forms (ones that he sees as being mostly grounded in tradition and preservation) with more popular art forms that he thinks audiences are still willing to go out of their way to see if it truly excites them (and he is doing his best to excite them). Yes, it was phrased in a clunky, even provocative way, but I think its important that we are able to be normal about this. No, people (the general public) do not care about ballet or opera. That is not a value judgement of the art, artists, or fans of these art forms. It is a statement of fact, albeit bluntly put.

The only question that remains with this first complaint (People do care about ballet and opera) is whether those declaring it and echoing it are truly misunderstanding or engaging in a bit of strategic obtusity.

Getting on with the second complaint (Chalamet is shitting on two art forms that are many hundreds of years old, and he has no business doing such a thing), I can offer only scrunched eyebrows, a cocked head, and an earnest look of bewilderment. Did we read the same quote? “That’s not a shot,” said McConaughey. I’m going to have to agree.

First, one must accept my argument above: Timothée Chalamet did not make a value judgement; he accurately described the attitudes of the general public and spoke honestly about how he enjoys being part of an industry still at the forefront of popular culture. If anything, it seems to be a truly candid glimpse into the mind of a film actor who loves film acting. I feel real confusion over the amount of people this comment seemed to piss off. Did Whoopi Goldberg really, truly miss his point? McConaughey didn’t. I didn’t. I don’t think Goldberg did either, and I think her framing comment reveals more about this previously referenced obtusity.

Before responding directly to the BOOC, Goldberg stated that Chalamet “took some heat during his award season campaign for comments hyping up his talent”. This is pretty much true. It started, in my opinion, with his basically well-received 2025 SAG Awards speech wherein he stated that he is in pursuit of greatness and refused to downplay the amount of work he put in. A few clips followed thereafter. Chalamet spoke about how he doesn’t want people to take how hard he works for granted, about how his recent output has been “top level shit”, and about how he hopes audiences appreciate the high level of talent being put on screen in Marty Supreme. That was when things started to turn. He had a featured verse in British rapper EsDeeKid’s “4 Raws Remix” and appeared in the accompanying music video. This move was received with… mixed reviews. Interestingly, the complaints seemed to focus on the idea that Chalamet was an artsy, French-speaking, NYU alum, and a rap song felt incongruous with this image he had built for himself.

Pre-BOOC, Chalamet was already flying dangerously close to the sun. People were already interested in finding something they could condemn Chalamet for, and these comments filled that role perfectly.

The infuriated reactions were, oftentimes, cheap virtue signaling. Doja Cat, to her great credit, posted a follow up TikTok in which she engaged in some introspection and honesty. “I don’t know anything about opera. I don’t know anything about ballet, and I’ve never been to either,” she states candidly. She is open about the fact that she was looking for an opportunity to pat herself on the back, and she took it. She also touches on the fact that many industry experts have essentially agreed with Chalamet’s bluntly put core argument. The vitriol allowed people a righteous way to proudly state that they care about opera and ballet. Congratulations! For the industry insiders, it allowed them a perfect moment of victimization, wherein the biggest Hollywood actor in the world attacked their ancient, precious art form.

Timothée Chalamet had gone through what many celebrities, influencers, and even politicians go through online. He was a victim of his own success. He outstayed his welcome as white boy of the month.

Older titans of his industry (see Curtis or Goldberg) either seized this opportunity or fell into the very basic internet trap that all of us seem to hate despite rarely being able to resist: Out of a 90-minute conversation, a 15-second clip was taken and interpreted in the least charitable way possible. “I see what you’re saying”, said McConaughey. It now seems that more people are coming around to the truth of the matter. The backlash to the backlash, as they say, is in full swing. I suppose I’m part of it.

So, uh, to close, I’m not mad. I’m disappointed. Disappointed that I once again watched the internet (fueled by major news outlets and personalities) lose their minds over a basically innocuous comment. Disappointed that we still haven’t figured out how to stop doing that. Perhaps most importantly, I am disappointed in myself for writing 2,000 words about the whole thing.
