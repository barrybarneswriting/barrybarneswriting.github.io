title: "The Compass"
date: 2026-01-14
section: fiction
---

Luopon watched the people sitting in her wagon, thinking only of possibility, for there was nothing else. Sula, the spirited older woman who had become something of a first mate to Luopon, could very well be her mother. They even shared the same pale, washed-out hair, lighter than one would expect for women of their complexions. Another passenger, younger than Sula but older than Luopon, had assumed a maternal role for the youngest two travelers among them, a boy and a girl, neither more than ten years old. Luopon thought it unlikely that the woman was, in fact, the children’s mother. Her nose pointed where the boy’s was quite round, and her fine, black hair seemed unrelated to the girl’s thick orange curls.

It didn’t matter. It didn’t matter to the boy or to the girl or to their “mother”, but it also very truly did not matter. This journey, in a certain sense, will have never happened for the travelers upon its completion, assuming the party’s consensus could be trusted. Just as they seemed to appear in the Waste nine days ago, physically undisturbed but missing that sacred data humanity calls their identity, they would eventually appear at their destination, knowing that they had completed their journey but without recollection of who they were during their twelve-some-odd days spent in the barren lands between. Luopon and the passengers of the creaking wagon all agreed on this. They knew it to be true as Luopon knew that she must lead them. No one else could, and, after all, she would be following her Compass to their destination either way. It did not matter if others trailed behind her.

“If I didn’t know you better, I’d think you were getting bored!”

Luopon smiled at Sula. She sat next to her at the front of the wagon, dealing with cranky passengers and occasionally adjusting the horses’ equipment.

“With scenery like this?” Luopon swung her upturned palm in a semi-circle, highlighting the endless rocky, white landscape.

“Well”, said Sula, grunting as she leaned forward to tighten a strap on one of their horses. “Yelo swears he spotted a tree while exploring after we made camp last night. That’ll be exciting”.

“We can all be thankful that I’m sitting in the driver’s seat and not Yelo. There are no trees in the Waste. If there were, I would know. So long as they were living. Just chalk and stone for us, I think”.

“And chasms!” added Sula.

“Chasm”, replied Luopon, stressing the singular form of the word. “With any luck, the remainder of our journey will be entirely chasmless”.

Sula looked like she was about to argue, but shrugged and shouted for the rest of their party to prepare to stop and set up camp. The first hints of orange sunlight were peeking over the gentle slopes of a distant drumlin field. Luopon felt as though she could already feel the radiation on her face and neck, warming her body and threatening to scorch her flesh if she stayed exposed for much longer. The thin air of the Waste provided nothing by way of slowing down that great star’s rays. Luckily, the severe aridity of the climate and bright white landscape meant that heat did not linger so much at night. This allowed Luopon’s party to hide themselves away under thick canvas sheets during the burning daylight hours and travel during the warm but survivable night.

Funny, how she knew all this and could not say for certain if her mother, brother, or lover was a member of this party. She knew even that she may not have a brother or a lover, but, after a moment’s pondering, concluded that she certainly had a mother. Well, that’s something, she thought. Right now, she may be nobody to herself, but she was something to somebody. The sound of a young woman’s scream reminded her that, to these people, she was in charge. She made eye contact with Sula. The older woman sighed, rolled her eyes, and followed behind Luopon as she marched towards the source of the sound.

* * *

Luopon found a tearful woman in the arms of the one who had anointed herself mother of the young children. The woman was taking in slow, deep breaths, clearly trying to calm herself. The mother dabbed at a small cut just to the left of the woman’s temple. The rest of the travelers were gathered around them in a sort of defensive formation. Luopon followed their eyes to a man sitting on the white ground, head in his hands. It was Yelo, the jovial, goofy man who constantly announced the wondrous (and fictional) discoveries he could feel were just beyond the horizon. He was sobbing. He made no attempt to stop and, upon laying eyes on Luopon, wailed louder into the growing light.

Luopon looked again to the mother who was busy scowling at the crying man. She pushed through the crowd and stopped several feet in front of Yelo.

“Yelo! What did you do?” The intensity of her voice surprised even her. To her dismay, she felt the band of travelers cringe away from her, becoming very quiet and very still. Yelo reacted similarly. Luopon looked at the quivering man staring back at her with wide eyes and quick breaths and felt suddenly guilty. She glanced back at the crowd, swore to herself, and knelt down closer to Yelo.

“Yelo, it’s okay. Just explain what happened and do it quickly. The sun is rising, and we need to make camp.”

“Kara… I- I know her,” said Yelo, fighting through still uneven breaths.

“Yelo, you don’t know Kara. Why is she bleeding?”

“I do know her! As sure as I know that the sun burns our skin and that I must drink water to live, I know her. I knew her before, and I know her now, and I will know her when my body breathes its last breath. We loved each other. We love each other. She just needs to remember.”

His words came like an ocean wave, beginning in a great crash and ending with little more than a whimper.

“Can’t you tell her? You know the Waste; you must know more than you have told us.”

Luopon sighed.

“I don’t know the Waste any more than you do, Yelo. I have a feeling. A sense for things, for life. In front of me, I feel your presence. I could tell you that a group of living things is standing just behind me, even if I had not seen or heard them. And out there, not too much further now, waits an entire city. More beings than I could count in a year. I know what you know. I am just a Compass”.

“But I know her,” whined Yelo. “She knows me too! She must!”

“Yelo, you know as well as I do that you cannot possibly know Kara. You can’t tell me if you had a brother, what year you were born, or if you’ve ever owned a pet. You can’t know her.”

“I-”

“Yelo!”

This time, Luopon called upon that grating intensity with purpose. Yelo cowered back from her, as if her face were a freshly fed furnace.

“What did you do?”

The man resumed his wailing, curling up uselessly on the ground. Luopon took a breath and turned towards Kara. She pulled away from the mother’s arms and stepped forward.

“I don’t know him.”

“Yes. Obviously.”

“But, this wasn’t his fault,” said Kara, gesturing towards her temple.

“He came to me, trying to tell me all this. He was upset, and he upset me, but he wasn’t violent. I was flustered and I slipped on a rock and banged my head on that boulder.”

The woman who had been cradling her nodded begrudgingly at Luopon, confirming the tale. Kara walked forward to kneel next to Yelo.

“It’s going to be all right, honey. Once we get where we’re going, none of this will have even happened. Don’t beat yourself up.”

“You- but- how could you not remember,” whimpered Yelo.

Kara looked to the Compass for guidance, but Luopon simply shook her head. As much as she would like to help Yelo, she was not equipped for this. She wondered if she should sneak off at the first sign of dusk. Looking to the mother holding her two children tight, Luopon knew she could not. She needn’t lead the troubled travelers, but she would guide them. For now.

“Sula!” called Luopon.

“Yes boss?” answered Sula.

“Can you sort him out?” asked Luopon, looking toward the man still crumpled on the ground. “And keep him away from Kara if you can,” she said, quieter. “We don’t need any more conflict this close to our destination.”

Sula nodded, knelt down at Yelo’s side, whispered something in his ear, and gently helped him to his feet.

“The rest of you,” called Luopon, squinting into the ever-brighter horizon, “break down that wagon and set up the canvas. At this point, we’re measuring minutes in layers of skin – so make it quick.”

* * *

The day passed without issue. It was hot underneath the canvasses, though easily thirty degrees cooler than the outside. Luopon slept restlessly and did not dream. She wondered if the other travelers dreamed. If they did, they must be of white rock and chalky hands. What other memories would shape such visions out here? Each other, thought Luopon. Perhaps they dream of each other.

After the sun had disappeared beyond the horizon, the travelers dismantled their canvas tents. The support poles became the bows upon the wagon bed, and the canvas sheets were fastened to these. Covering the wagon served little purpose in the dark. Luopon figured that most of her party found walls preferable to staring into the unending wilderness.

Luopon sat at the front of the wagon, reins in hand. Sula emerged from behind the wagon and looked up at her. The creases of the woman’s face looked deeper than usual. A distinct triangle had formed in the flesh between her eyebrows.

“Yelo is gone,” she said.

“Gone?” asked Luopon.

“He left. Must have. He was pretty torn up about his little incident yesterday. Kept repeating that he knew Kara and that she knew him. Blamed himself for her injury too. Rightly so if you ask me, but that’s beside the point”.

Luopon wondered briefly if the man had any chance of finding his own way.

“Did he take anything important?”

“No, not really. There’s something to be thankful for. Took his own pack, one of the extra support poles, a bit of canvas, and some rope. Nothing that’ll cause problems for us”.

“Yes. That is something to be thankful for.” Luopon’s voice trailed off, sounding decidedly ungrateful.

“Luo, it wasn’t your fault. I’m sure you feel it too. To be on a journey, not knowing who you are, with strangers who don’t know who they are. It\'s a lot. Too much for some people. And besides, maybe he’ll make it.”

“Maybe,” said Luopon.

Not wishing to explore her thoughts on this any further, Luopon shouted that the party was moving once more and coaxed the horses forward into the white Waste.

Not an hour into the night’s journey, Sula sat forward in her seat next to Luopon. She leaned until Luopon thought she would fall out of the wagon and underneath its wheels.

“What is it, Sula?” asked Luopon, pulling the older woman back into her seat and squinting into the night.

“I’ll be damned,” said Sula quietly.

“Sula?” repeated Luopon. She saw it then. A tree, or something shaped very much like a tree. It was distant. A tree-shaped smudge on the edge of the horizon, black and without leaves. Something, Luopon could not tell what at this distance, hung from an outstretched branch.

Sula turned to Luopon, grinning.

“That, Little Luo, is life!”

Luopon smiled back.

“Not life, exactly. I can tell you that tree is long dead, but still, it must be a good sign. And that decoration hanging from the largest of the branches; someone hung it there. Someone must have been through here, another traveler maybe. Someone leaving signs for people like us! We must be close”.

Sula gripped Luopon’s shoulder warmly and stuck her head into the wagon to make the announcement. Luopon felt some of the tension in her melt away. She listened to the excited noises from the travelers. In twos and threes, they stuck their head between the canvas flaps and saw what Sula had seen. Lupon did not think such a pleasant mood had taken their party during the whole of their trip. She felt a pang of dread at this. To think that these people, or the way she knew these people, would be wiped away in a few short days was painful. It had been nice to watch people grow close to each other during their journey. She knew that, just like her, the other travelers had family waiting for them, and it felt wrong to feel anything other than excitement at the prospect of returning memories and families reunited. Luopon just wished there was some way she didn’t have to lose her identity, and those relationships contained within, all over again.

They drew closer to the tree, still distant, but the picture became clearer now. The tree’s bark was white, a few shades darker than the surrounding earth and broken up by patches of gray where the tree’s flesh had been laid bare. There were no leaves across its long, flat crown. Something did hang from the largest of the branches. Luopon squinted and hoped against hope that the smudge would not sharpen into what she now believed she was looking at. She turned to Sula. The woman gazed into the horizon, grim.

“Luo,” she rasped. “Is that…”

“Stay here,” ordered Luopon. “And don’t let anyone out of the wagon”.

Luopon’s feet hit the ground hard as she leapt out of the driver’s seat. She trudged slowly, pointedly towards the tree, listening to the crunch of stone on stone beneath her. Though the sky was painted with countless stars, the darkness flattened out any details of far away objects. The young woman could now make out two distinct silhouettes: the rigid form of the lanky, dead tree and the shape of man hanging from a few feet of rope. The man swayed casually in the night’s breeze.

It was impossible to tell how old the body was without drawing closer. Luopon knew that, despite being downwind of the corpse, there would be no scent of putrefaction. Microbial life was limited in the Waste. Bodies did not bloat. They did not rot. Travelers lost to the Waste did not become skeletons. Rather, their skin dried out and pulled taught across their bones. A corpse could rest in the Waste for millenia, their skin turned to leather but otherwise intact. Luopon prepared to look into the eyes of a mummy.

Yelo’s eyes stared back at her. Luopon recoiled, Yelo’s corpse a physical force pushing her away. She gathered herself and looked again. She now noticed the plain white clothing, the boots, same as hers, and the close-cropped widow’s peak. Mustering whatever resolve she had left, Luopon looked again at the man’s face. Not a day earlier, Yelo had the barest hints of wrinkles around his eyes and the corners of his mouth. Sula’s voice came to mind: You should hope for lines like these. Means you’ve lived. Means you laughed when you were tickled and cried when you were sad. Yelo’s lines had been erased, his skin already drying out, tightening, beginning to draw taught along his jaw and cheekbones.

Luopon thought of the man’s giddy laughter and absurd declarations of fantastic beasts just out of sight for everyone but him. She thought of the pain in his voice. I know her. Of how he was unable to even look at the bleeding Kara. She knows me too. She thought of her own insistence that he was wrong. She remembered how he flinched back from her when she yelled. She remembered conjuring that voice a second time, intending to frighten an already terrified man into obedience.

Bile rose in Luopon’s throat. Her fingertips burned, and she was suddenly unsteady on her feet. The white stone beneath her seemed to slide away. Luopon tumbled backwards, expecting jagged rock but landing instead against a short, strong body. Sula\'s hands gripped Luopon’s shoulders and kept her upright.

She spoke quietly, almost cooing.

“Close your eyes, Little Luo. Deep breaths now”.

Luopon felt hot tears on her cheeks. They tickled her jaw as the droplets ran down to her chin and dripped onto the stone beneath her. Sula dabbed at the streaks with her shirt.

“Luopon, do not let them see you cry”.

“It’s Yelo”, said Luopon, her voice raw with emotion but strong, steady.

“I know, Luo, but the rest of them don’t,” Sula nodded slightly in the direction of the wagon. “How do you expect the others to react to something like this? You need to help them.”

There was a sudden heat in Luopon’s belly, traveling quickly to her eyes and ears. She fought down the urge to shove Sula away, to scream. Instead, her fingers dug into Sula’s wrists. Her words came in a hoarse whisper.

“I don’t want to.”

Sula didn’t react.

“I was never interested in leading them, Sula. They’re more than welcome to follow me, but I don’t know how to help them. I was too harsh with Yelo.” Luopon’s voice cracked at this. “I didn’t know what to do”.

“Yelo lost his own battle, Luo. Take a deep breath, wipe your eyes, and tell them that body has been there for a hundred years. We’ll say we need to take a wide berth around so as not to frighten any of the children.”

Luopon tilted her head back and breathed deeply through her nose. Then, she nodded once. She walked a few paces ahead of Sula back to the cart and quietly informed the adults that they would be taking a wide path around the tree. The corpse had been hanging there for at least a decade. It was a grisly sight, and the children need not be subjected to it.

* * *

The sun would not rise for an hour or two longer, but Luopon ordered camp to be made early. Sula, it seemed, had been right. At least one more chasm stood between the travelers and their destination. Their wagon contained the necessary materials to construct a sturdy, if humble, bridge, but Luopon was not in the mood to cross a chasm. She could use an extra bit of rest.

Sula oversaw the breaking down of the wagon bed and the erection of the canvas tents. Luopon wandered a few dozen paces closer to the chasm, trying to determine exactly how much longer she must spend in the Waste. She focused on that strange ability which had put her in charge of these people. It was more ephemeral than flexing a muscle. It was like squinting into a distant landscape or trying to hear a faint melody on the wind. The Compass focused on that jumbled beacon of life, amorphous but distinct and pulsing with thousands of lives. She stood on the precipice of the chasm. It was jagged, dark, and deeper than Luopon cared to consider. Lost in her meditation, she failed to notice a presence approaching from behind until they were mere inches away.

Luopon yelped and whirled. Kara jumped backwards and let out a yelp of her own.

“Kara!” said Luopon, too loudly.

“Hi, Luopon,” Kara spoke quietly, and her face settled into deep, concerned lines. They reminded Luopon of the chasm before her.

“Is something wrong?” asked Luopon.

“Well,” the woman looked to the ground. “It’s about Yelo.”

“Yes. I was sorry he left. This whole journey, it’s hard. Hard for everyone.” Luopon kept her voice as steady as she could. She should be troubled, but she could not show panic or nerves.

“I was wondering if you could find him.”

“Find him?” Luopon swept her eyes across the vast wilderness surrounding them.

“I don’t want to bother you, but I was thinking. As far as I’m aware, nobody packed any cherry pies for our journey. But if someone, somehow, were to be baking a cherry pie out here, and if I were to be standing near their oven, I’m sure I could identify their creation using just my nose. And if someone had been standing near a fire, I could tell you if they were burning Clawtree or Greasewood, despite us avoiding the only tree we’ve come across during our travels.”

“Kara, I-”

“So, maybe not everything is lost when we enter the Waste. Maybe Yelo really did know something. And maybe you could find him. I imagine that he might have a unique scent to him that you could use to track him down.”

“Kara, I don’t smell people. That’s not how it works.”

“Dammit, I know that’s not how it works. It’s a metaphor,” her voice took on a bit of edge now. Luopon winced. She was being obtuse, talking down to a woman who was simply asking for help.

“I’m sorry. Of course it was a metaphor. Still, Kara, I don’t think I can do what you’re asking.”

“Can’t?” asked Kara.

“Well-”

“Everything just feels the same to you? Sula, myself, the children?” Anger continued to rise in Kara’s voice. Luopon knew the question was practically rhetorical, but she tried to invent a satisfactory answer anyway.

“People do have vaguely distinct energies. The trouble locating Yelo would be his distance from us now. If I try to reach out for him, his signal would be too muddled by our own party and the distance between us.”

“You’re taking us to some great far away city, aren’t you? You don’t seem to have a problem with that.”

“Kara, if I could bring Yelo back, I would.” Luopon fought to keep her voice steady.

“Can’t you at least try?” Kara’s voice broke. “He left because of me, and now he’s going to get himself killed.”

“That’s not true, Kara. He lost-”

“He lost his own battle.”

A new voice. Male, aged, with an accent Luopon didn’t recognize. She spun left, her back still to the chasm, and saw a man dressed in plain white clothing. He was old, older than any one of Luopon’s party. Dark circles under his eyes dominated his visage, but he looked at the two women with a bewildering warmth.

“I’m very sorry to interrupt, Ms. Luopon, but I hoped to speak with you before you ventured across this chasm.”

* * *

Luopon instinctively put herself between the strange old man and Kara. Did he overhear Kara speak her name? It was the only explanation. She opened her mouth to speak. Kara beat her to it.

“Who are you?” demanded the young woman.

The man smiled and held out his hands, palms to the sky.

“You should call me Veren. My father’s name was also Veren, and my mother’s name was Ada. I have no siblings. More cousins than I can count, though.”

He took a step away from the chasm, moving to speak to Kara more directly.

“I enjoy fishing. My favorite playwright is named Aster Baird. I have come to help”.

Veren took another slow step towards Luopon and Kara.

“Now, who are you?”

“I’m Kara,” she responded. The words came out feebly, as if they knew themselves to be unsatisfactory. “How do you know all those things about yourself? Out here, I mean.”

“Well, Kara, I know lots of things.”

Veren then whipped a fold of his robe aside and reached to draw something from his belt. Luopon reacted instinctively. Her right hand shot forward, wrapping around the elderly man’s frail wrist and pulling his dominant arm towards her. She pressed her left elbow against the man’s neck simultaneously, firm, but without enough pressure to cause any damage. His knife hand dangled in the direction of the chasm. Veren grunted but made no real attempt to resist.

“Drop it”, ordered Luopon.

The old man croaked, “Ms. Luopon, if you would just-”

“Luopon, it’s ok,” said Kara. “It’s not a weapon”.

Luopon removed her elbow from the man’s neck, but kept her grip on his right hand. She glanced at the object he was holding. It appeared to be made of wood, long and skinny. It was hollow. Holes had been drilled across its top and through either end.

“A flute?” asked Luopon.

“Yes, a flute,” Veren snatched his hand away from Luopon. “For Kara”.

“What am I supposed to do with a flute?” asked Kara. Her words were harsh, but they came out slowly, dreamily.

‘See for yourself”, replied Veren.

He stepped around Luopon once more and raised the flute to Kara. Delicately, she reached out and plucked it from his hands. She turned it over in her hands, once, twice, and then put the tapered end to her lips.

A note rang out into the night. Then another. Then another. Melancholic tones flowed from the instrument and echoed off the walls of the great chasm below. Luopon watched her fingers move across the wood, fumbling at first though rapidly picking up speed. She thought of lovers reunited. Kara’s eyes were closed now. She returned to that same sad motif that begun her song. Though unable to recall any music she knew prior to this journey, Luopon could tell that this melody was not particularly complex. It was not the speed of Kara’s fingers or the precision of her breaths that impacted Luopon so. Kara seemed, Luopon thought, whole. This may have been the first time in their twelve day journey that Luopon had seen a person in their entirety. Not a glimpse of who they might be or a void where memories should have gone. This little wooden flute completed Kara. Somehow, this Veren had known it would.

The final notes of Kara’s song glided to their conclusion, and Luopon saw that tears now flowed from the woman’s dark eyes. Veren offered her an orange silk handkerchief. She accepted, cleared her throat, and looked to the old man. Her eyes were slightly swollen, but she smiled.

“Thank you”.

“Of course, my dear”.

Luopon stepped between them.

“Hold on. Sharing your parents’ names is one thing, but how could you know that Kara plays the flute? Why would you know that? Who are you?”

“Ms. Luopon, my name is Veren, and, at my age, I fear the sun would rise and burn us all before I could share my life story.”

“What else do you know about Kara?”

The man bowed his head slightly.

“Not that much more, but enough. The same goes for all your journeymen”.

“You know who we are?” asked Luopon, then quickly correcting herself, “Or- were?”

“Luopon,” the man sighed deeply and looked into Luopon’s eyes, “I’ve come to help”.

Whatever reaction he expected, the two women did not offer it.

“I offered Kara a flute as evidence of my credentials. It would not only be infeasible for me to bring an item to prove to each one of your passengers of the validity of my knowledge of them – it would be a waste of time. As for you, Ms. Luopon, I can tell you that you have no family on this trip. No friends, no lovers, nobody”.

Luopon felt spiteful words on her tongue and resisted the impulse. She had not really expected these people to be her family, but such a willful delusion was useful on a journey like theirs.

“Why are you telling me this?” she asked.

“Because I value honesty, and because I’m going to ask you to do something that you aren’t going to like very much”.

“If you think you’re about to tell us to turn around, I’d remind you which of us is standing closer to that chasm”.

Veren chuckled at that.

“That would certainly be a change of pace. No, not turn around. Just, change course”.

“Change course? Are you a Compass?”

“If only it were so. I am no Compass. I simply organize the movements of people out from whence they came and into their new homes. I know that you are most probably being drawn to a city we call Oprindell. This makes sense, as it is without contest the largest and most densely populated city in the East. Unfortunately, this is not your proper destination. There is another city, smaller, slightly more distant to the southeast of Oprindell. It is called Tarrasetan, and this is where you must lead your fellow travelers”.

Luopon dug her feet into the chalky rock beneath her and closed her eyes. She felt Kara and this stranger beside her. A little ways away, she felt Sula and a dozen other lives. In the opposite direction, she felt that roaring fire of life, Oprindell, as Veren called it. It could overwhelm her if she lingered for too long, so she did not. She tried to compartmentalize the great city. She imagined strong walls surrounding the energy, and then pictured herself soaring over these walls, peering beyond the cacophony. There. Maybe as far from the great city and they were from it now. Life. Compared to the Waste, a staggering wealth of it.

It was a strange thing, to feel another grouping of so many lives. Though totally distinct from Oprindell, it would be impossible to say why. Thousands of people, plants and animals, each with their distinct personalities and purpose. Though not offensive, this second city did not call to her. Oprindell, something therein, wanted her to get there even more badly than she wanted to be there. She did not feel the same from Tarrasetan. Still, Veren had provided one more piece of evidence to suggest that he was being honest with them.

Luopon zoomed back out from Tarrasetan, past Oprindell, across the Waste, and back to where she stood. She opened her eyes and felt a presence behind their trio approaching from the wagon camp.

“Hey there, Little Luo. Aren’t you going to introduce me to your new friend?”

“This is Veren. His father was a playwright. He says we are going the wrong way”.

Luopon was glad to have Sula, surefooted Sula, at her side.

“Well, Veren, we appreciate your input and all, but we already have a Compass. I guess you could say we aren’t in the market for another. You’re welcome to join us, though. Isn’t that right Luo?”

“He’s not a Compass,” responded Luopon.

“He knows things,” murmured Kara.

“Knows things?” asked Sula. “Like what? Like his father was a playwright? No offense, sir, but I could very well tell you my father was the high governor of Oprindell. Would you believe me?”

“No,” said Kara, holding up the flute. “He knew that I could play this. I didn’t even know I could play it, but it’s like I’ve remembered a part of myself”.

Luopon watched Sula, hoping she would know the right thing to say. With a quip and a jab, she would set everyone straight, and they could all continue on their way to the great, beckoning city.

“Lucky guess?” offered Sula, sheepishly.

“No,” sighed Luopon. “He knew about a city, different from the one we are going to. I can feel it just beyond our original destination. To the southeast. He knows things.”

“Well, why does he think we need to change course?”

“He”, interrupted Veren, “informed the Compass Luopon that she was meant to bring these people to Tarrasetan. Your families are waiting for you”.

“It doesn’t feel right, Sula” said Luopon. “Oprindell is calling me. The city, it’s like it knows I’m out here, and it wants me to find it. We can’t change course just because an old man with a few tricks tells us to”.

“For what it’s worth”, said Sula with a smile, “a few tricks has gotten this old woman pretty far”.

Sula’s joke was met with silence. Luopon, for once, did not feel more secure for it. She looked at Kara who met her gaze with an expression Luopon could not place. Contempt? No. Distrust, perhaps. Veren looked casually over the chasm’s pit as if nothing very interesting was happening at all.

Sula cleared her throat. “Why don’t we bring this to the rest of them? We can talk it over and decide as a group”.

“No”, said Luopon firmly.

“Luo-”

“We are not bringing this to the rest of them. I’m the leader, aren’t I? If we tell the others, they will insist that we go with the wizened desert man who purports to know their every secret. No one else needs to know. They will only be upset when I refuse their demands”.

“They have a right to know!” said Kara.

Luopon laughed. She couldn’t help herself, and a single, venomous bark escaped her throat. She thought of Yelo, of his too-tight flesh clinging so desperately to the bones of his face. Regaining her composure, she looked to Sula. The old woman was looking at her cautiously and offered a warning through a near imperceptible shaking of her head.

Veren moved closer to Kara and said, “Ms. Kara is right, of course. You may be the leader, Luopon, but you are no king. It is only right that you let me speak to the rest of your party”.

“Sometimes,” said Luopon, “not everyone needs to know everything. If any of you,” she pointed her finger wildly at the three, “say anything, I won’t lead you any further”.

It was an empty threat. Luopon knew so and knew that Sula, Kara, and Veren probably understood the same. She would not harm them. If she booted them from the party, they would simply follow from a distance. In the deadly sunlight, neither she nor the rest of the travelers would be able to deny them a canvas tent. She had no means by which to enforce her authority aside from giving up the journey herself. And that would not happen.

Luopon turned towards the array of canvas tents and moved to leave the other three standing on the cliffside. Veren’s voice called out to her once more, brooding.

“This is quite unlike you, Ms. Luopon”.

“I don’t believe you,” she shouted back.

Luopon hurried towards her tent, feeling impotent once again. She believed him. She had to believe at least parts of what he said. It was obviously not dumb luck that he provided the very instrument Kara was so well trained in. He knew about that other city out there, a significant grouping of life that even Luopon had been previously ignorant of. But Veren was not a Compass. He couldn’t feel the pull of Oprindell. The immediate consequence of this journey was that of a gaping void where one’s memories should be. When Luopon was thirsty, she sought water, and this feeling, making it to Oprindell, would nourish her damaged mind. Her absent memories need no longer be absent, so long as she could find that blazing beacon of life.

Pulling open the canvas flap, Luopon entered her tent and sat on her thin bedroll. She thought then of Kara and of the ways her fingers moved so deftly across the wooden flute. Those fingers moved with the precision of hundreds, perhaps thousands, of hours of practice. Kara must have had a teacher. She was young enough that a teacher must have been paid for by her parents or some other responsible party. She had probably played before friends and family, each time evoking that same melancholy in her audience.

Was Kara truly meant for Tarrasetan? If she was, if there was even a chance that her parents or anyone else who loved her were in that city, how could Luopon refuse to guide her? Luopon resented the task. She had her own possibilities to chase.

There was a rustling at the opening of the tent, and Sula’s intelligent eyes peered through the slit.

“Hey there, Little Luo. Mind if I come in?”

Luopon, despite herself, felt a wash of relief upon seeing the older woman.

“Sure.”

Luopon’s tent was smaller than the others and by far the most desirable, for it slept only one. The other travelers were two or three to a tent. Even Sula, with her less than imposing stature, contorted her body to squeeze through to Luopon. She delicately set herself down beside her Compass and let out a sigh that Luopon thought to be wholly unrelated to physical exertion.

“Listen, Luo. Don’t think I like this Veren guy any more than you do. He seems a touch enlightened for my tastes. Don’t worry about him. I lent him a bedroll, and he’s got an outcropping in the cliff face where I suppose he’s been waiting out the sun until we got to him. He’s not coming back to camp until we say so, and he’s won’t say anything to anybody”.

“And Kara?”

“Well, I don’t think she’ll say anything either, but I can’t make that a guarantee in good conscience. She’s angry, Luo. She thinks you’re keeping her from her home”.

Luopon groaned and put her head in her hands.

“I was considering the possibility.”

“That she thinks you’re keeping her from home?”

“That I am keeping her from home! I don’t know! I don’t know anymore than the rest of you. People seem to forget that. Hell, I sometimes forget that. Who am I to question this mysterious, all-knowing desert man?”

“I’d trust your judgement over just about anyone. Whatever decision you make, you won’t get any flak from me.”

Luopon nodded and sat for a moment, thinking. Sula seemed content with this and sat similarly still. If members of their party really belonged in Tarrasetan (Luopon accepted now that this was almost certainly the case) she had to take them there. Her conscience wouldn’t allow her to do otherwise. No memories, but her conscience was still there. She wondered how much of her personality made it through the transition to the Waste. Was she meaningfully different after entering this place? Would she be meaningfully different from that past version of herself when she arrived in Oprindell?

Something dawned on her then.

“Oh!” she exclaimed, gripping Sula’s shoulders.

“Luo?”

“This is how it was supposed to be! The whole time, before we left. This is how it was supposed to be.”

“I’m not sure I catch your meaning”, said Sula, that familiar triangle appearing between her eyebrows once more.

“It’s okay, Sula. I need to get my thoughts together. You should get back to your tent before the sun gets any higher. I’ll be over at dusk.”

The triangle between Sula’s eyebrows deepened, but her tone was light.

“Whatever you say, Compass Luopon”, Sula’s voice deepened as she mimicked Veren’s strict timbre.

Both women laughed, and Luopon stretched out on her cot as Sula pulled the canvas sheets closed behind her. How hadn’t she seen it before? How had nobody seen it? The solution seemed so glaringly obvious, it felt almost as though it had been snatched from her mind just like her memories.

* * *

As soon as it was cool enough to step outside, Luopon hurried to Sula’s tent. She had gathered her thoughts before she slept and felt confident that she could make everybody happy, including herself.

Sula opened the canvas flap before Luopon said a word. Luopon stepped inside and saw that the other cots were empty.

“What happened to your bunkmates?”

“I sent them out already. Told them I had a very important meeting with our leader, and that an extra coffee ration would be theirs if they got an early start on assembling the cart. Now, care to share that epiphany of yours?”

Luopon sat on the cot closest to the door.

“I’ve been thinking. We wouldn’t all be out here together if there wasn’t a plan. I can do both. I can lead us to Tarrasetan, restock my supplies, and travel to Oprindell myself. It would only add five or six days to my journey in total”.

“Luo, I don’t know”. Sula frowned deeply.

“What’s the problem? This works! I can’t believe we didn’t think of it before. It doesn’t have to be one or the other”.

“What if it doesn’t work like that? What if something happens? More chasms. Or some kind of great beast. Or strangers less friendly than old Veren. You can’t be out here on your own. It might feel safe enough with a dozen other people, but by yourself”, she shook her head. “Anything could happen.”

Luopon heard the words, but her mind was made up. She squeezed the older woman’s shoulder again.

“It’s okay, Sula. I can do this”.

With that, Luopon pulled the tent flaps open and strode out into the night. She would go speak with Veren, then with Kara. They would need to tell the rest of the party in a way that didn’t confuse or frighten anyone. Then again, Luopon was the Compass. The group would follow her without many questions. Would it be easier to simply tell them that the journey was going to take a few more days than she previously thought? It would be, but Luopon didn’t need to treat them like children. She might be their Compass, but that didn’t make her better than them. The group had a right to know everything she did.

A vision of too-tight skin drawn across flesh flashed through her mind. She winced and reevaluated her position. Maybe not a right, and maybe not everything. There was a certain setting apart that her position in the group gave her. She was not better than them, but she was uniquely positioned to make decisions that would make the journey easier for everyone. This responsibility had fallen to her, and Luopon would fulfill it to the best of her abilities. The group needn’t know about poor Yelo. The group would learn about Veren and Tarrasetan and their change in course. This was her decision.

Luopon approached the chasm. Standing half a dozen feet back, she called out.

“Veren!”

A moment passed. Two. Then, a wisp of pale, washed out hair appeared just beyond the cliff’s edge in front of where she stood. It looked much like hers, she realized. Below the wisp was Veren’s sun-worn face.

“Compass Luopon! Perfect timing”.

“I’ve decided what I’m going to do. We-”

“Not so hasty, Miss Luopon. Why don’t you come down, and we can have a proper conversation?”

Veren disappeared beyond the cliff face again, and Luopon rolled her eyes. He did seem prone to overcomplicating things. With a sigh, Luopon lowered herself carefully down the cliffside to the narrow rock outcropping below, trying her best to ignore the gray abyss below. The outcropping created a sort of path that led a few steps south to a sizable cave. Veren had taken a seat near the entrance. Beyond him sat Kara. She was crying, bitter, angry tears. Luopon’s heart ached for her momentarily. She thought she would never be going home. Luopon would fix this. She was feeling more confident about her position as a leader, and Kara would be thrilled to hear the new plan.

Luopon smiled sympathetically at Kara and moved to place her hand on the woman’s shoulder. Kara twisted violently out of reach and inhaled sharply. It sounded almost like a hiss.

“It’s alright, Kara. I’m taking you to Tarrasetan. We just need to get over this chasm, and-”

“Quiet!” Kara snapped.

Luopon reeled back against the opposite side of the cave. Kara watched her with hate in her eyes. What’s the problem? Isn’t this what she wanted? Luopon glanced at Veren. He was standing a few feet from Kara, staring soberly at the stone floor. His typically pompous demeanor had shrunken.

Oh no.

“Kara, I thought I was-”

“You knew!” Kara shrieked.

Luopon found herself at a loss for words.

“Poor Yelo. He didn’t have to go and do something like that. I never thought he would. I was just scared when he said all those things. I just didn’t remember. I still don’t remember.”

The fury in her voice had quickly collapsed into sobs.

“Kara, please. I thought I was making the best decision for everyone.”

Kara scoffed, the tears slowing.

“For you, maybe. It would have been real inconvenient for you to have everybody knowing that jolly old Yelo died like that. You should have told us. You might be our Compass, but you’re no leader.”

Luopon swallowed hard and fought back an urge to cry.

“I know.”

Kara stared at her a moment longer, burning hot anger behind those eyes. She pushed past Luopon to leave but paused at the mouth of the cave.

“I can’t wait to forget you.”

Luopon watched her deep brown hair disappear beyond the rock wall. A crushing guilt was settling on her, threatening to smother her. She whirled on Veren.

“What is wrong with you? I had everything figured out. I was going to make everyone happy! She wouldn’t even remember what happened! She still won’t. You took it upon yourself to cause her meaningless pain, and for what?”

Veren smoothed his blue suncloak against his hips and waist. Luopon realized that this appeared to be a change of clothes and wondered briefly how he possibly kept a wardrobe in a place like this.

“Compass Luopon, you come to realize certain things when you spend enough time in the Waste.” He sighed. “Let me ask you this: are you an entire person?”

“Of course I am.”

“Even without your memories? I mean, are you not, in a manner of speaking, barely a couple of weeks old? Not your language skills or your general understanding of things in this world, but in terms of experience. What makes you a whole person without a lifetime of history, relationships, and lessons learned?”

“I… I don’t know.”

“I don’t know either, Miss Luopon, but I happen to agree with you. Having no memories should not preclude you from being thought of or treated as a full human being. Similarly, just because you will inevitably forget that we ever had this conversation, it does not mean that I shouldn’t treat you with dignity and respect in the here and now.”

“Look, Veren, I understand your point. But you could be jeopardizing the well-being of this entire party. More than that, you’ve made Kara feel guilty over the death of someone she had no relationship to. You should have left it alone.”

“Guilt was not my objective, and I suspect if you examine your heart, you may find that you have superimposed your own emotions onto the legitimate grief of another. Kara, this version of Kara, had a right to know about Yelo.” The old man turned his gaze to the mouth of the cave, gazing wistfully. “They had been married only a few weeks. It is a terrible tragedy, but one that she has the right to mourn, no matter her memories.\"

Luopon felt the world spin. A chill ran across her body.

“Married?”

“I’m afraid so, Miss Luopon.”

That crushing, smothering weight had returned.

“You told her.”

“Of course I did.”

Luopon nodded numbly.

A moment of silence passed between them before Luopon heard a crunching of rocks outside the cavern. A familiar grunt echoed bounced off the cave walls. Luopon’s right hand had come to join them.

“Told you before, old man, but I’ll reiterate,” Sula’s voice bounced off the walls before she ever entered. “As far as the Waste goes, you’ve got a real lovely place here. Real lovely place. It’s…”

She rounded the corner and locked eyes with Luopon.

“Cozy,” she concluded weakly. “What’s wrong, Luo?”

Luopon relaxed somewhat at the sight of Sula, but that guilt remained. In Sula’s presence, it alchemized into something more akin to shame.

“Veren told Kara about Yelo.”

“Oh,” Sula cooed sympathetically. “Luo.”

“It’s okay. Let’s not. Not right now.”

Luopon couldn’t spare another thought on that subject just now.

“I’m going to take everyone to Tarrasetan. It would be wrong for me not to. As long as I don’t enter the city, I can retain my memories and make the journey back to Oprindell myself”.

A look passed between Sula and Veren. Quick, subtle, but distinctly something. As if noticing Luopon’s observation, Veren clicked his tongue sharply.

“Miss Luopon, I must say I’m frustrated that you still insist on visiting Oprindell, but it is commendable that you have found a way that still ends with your party making it home.”

Luopon grunted ambiguously, wondering about that look. Had the two spent more time speaking last night than Sula had let on? Was Luopon displaying some sort of youthful ignorance only and instantly recognizable to the more aged? Strange though it was, she couldn’t waste time wondering right now.

“Sula, get everyone moving and quickly. We’ve got a chasm to cross.”

* * *

A few days after their journey began, the travelers had come across the first chasm. It was not particularly wide, perhaps eight feet across. This was narrow enough for the horses and most abled-bodied among them to jump without issue, but far too wide for the old and very young. Accordingly, the party had closely followed the set of instructions found in the wagon detailing how a team could take it apart, construct it into a reinforced bridge, and put it back together again as a functioning wagon. This new chasm was, if anything, a bit longer than the first.

They unloaded the foodstuffs, water, and meager personal items into a heap on the stone. The canvas and bows were removed and set aside. Then came the axles, the tongue, the hounds, and the wagon bed. The boards of the wagon bed were inordinately bulky, crafted for this very purpose. The hounds were laid parallel across the gap, and the axles were placed diagonally across the hounds. Finally, the boards of the wagon bed were placed flush against each other, forming the platform for foot traffic.

Sula stayed with the horses while, in a single-file line, the elderly and very young crossed first. Luopon almost smiled at the anxiety on the Mother’s face, clutching the children as she tip-toed across the bridge. Anyone who was able carried provisions, belongings, and leftover wagon parts across the bridge. Sturdy though it was and wide enough for two people to cross at once, this part of the process took the longest. Luopon ordered that there be no heroes; nobody would carry more than one item, and if you felt even a shimmer of doubt, you would ask for assistance.

The first chasm crossing took nearly two hours by Luopon’s estimate. This time, the party spent no more than an hour about the task. They had admittedly diminished their supplies since that time, but Luopon thought it was their familiarity with the strategy and each other that drove this improved efficiency.

From there, the day was quiet. Sula sat beside Luopon as always, but there was a palpable absence of Sula’s sarcastic remarks and canned aphorisms. The pair rode in near silence. Those in the wagon acted much the same. Luopon figured Veren was keeping to himself and that the rest of her party was perfectly content pretending the strange desert man did not exist.

Two days passed like this, Luopon’s two-fold guilt weighing on her all the while. Veren, she had concluded, was correct. Luopon should not have kept Yelo’s fate to herself, especially after Kara handed her the perfect opportunity to come clean. Her husband! Freshly wed and promptly separated forever. One thought kept bouncing around Luopon’s mind.

Now she has to lose him twice.

There was nothing to be done about the primary cause of her guilt. Those cards had been dealt and played. The second part of Luopon’s guilt came from the change in Sula’s demeanor. She was being quiet, incredibly so by her standards, and Luopon knew why. Although it had been Luopon’s decision, Sula had counselled her not to share Yelo’s demise with the party. Now, she blamed herself for Luopon’s failure. Perhaps there was something to be done about this.

“Sula?”

The name left Luopon’s mouth with a quaver she wasn’t expecting.

“What is it, Luo?”

“I don’t blame you. I want you to know that it was my decision.”

Sula’s mouth flattened into a straight line, and her eyes danced around the barren landscape.

“For Yelo. I mean, for not telling anyone the truth about him. I know you gave me the best advice you could, and it was my own decision to make. I don’t want you blaming yourself for my own failures. I couldn’t have hoped for a more reliable friend to make this journey with.”

Sula exhaled, as if relieved.

“Oh, Luo,” she said. “Nothing to worry about on that front. You’re one hell of a Compass, and a better friend than I deserve.”

“Nonsense,” laughed Luopon.

“I mean it, Luo. We all think the world of you. If we had more time, I bet even Kara would come to understand.”

Luopon settled back into the bench, her world becoming a few shades brighter in light of Sula’s encouragement.

“Thanks, Sula.”

“Always, kid,” she let out a long sigh. “Anyways, how much further is this place. Can’t be far now, right?”

Luopon reached out, beyond the gently sloping drumlin they were navigating. She found that Sula was right. To the north, behind her, she felt Oprindell, that familiar, beckoning warmth. Beyond her laid the blazing pyre of Tarrasetan. No, beyond was the wrong word. It was close. Very close.

She almost shared this with Sula but figured it must be a fluke. Something in the stone barriers around them interfering with her abilities. If the city was as close as it felt, and it felt as though the fire of Tarrasetan could consume her at any moment, they would surely know it. They would hear the sounds or smell the scents of ten thousand people huddling together to escape the Waste. At the very least, they would see a city wall or other structures. Could the city be underground?

Her confusion was stamped out as they rounded the final curve of the drumlin. The Gate stood no more than a hundred paces in front of them. It was huge. Two columns of white wood wrapped intermittently with embossed black metal rose twenty meters into the clear night sky. The doors between these were styled similarly: white wood framed by black metal. These details were mere footnotes to the Gate’s most unusual attribute. It stood completely alone. This Gate, seemingly designed to keep all manner of foe from breaching it, didn’t seem to be protecting anything.

Luopon was snapped out of her wonder by the sound of boots landing hard on stone followed by a figure in maroon dashing past the wagon and towards the Gate. Kara. Luopon listened to the awed whispers of her fellow travelers while Kara pushed and pulled wildly against the Gate. She was soon joined by a stocky middle-aged man (Luopon had never caught this one’s name), but he found no more success than the girl.

Luopon pulled the wagon up to the Gate and hopped to the ground. The other travelers flooded out of the wagon and joined Kara and the man, pushing hopelessly against the doors. Luopon rounded the Gate and found that, indeed, the Gate stood alone in this Waste. Coming back around to the other side, she locked eyes with Sula, who had been watching the travelers struggle. Veren strolled out from behind the wagon and cleared his throat pointedly.

“Excuse me, everyone. If I might have a moment of your time, so as to enlighten you in regard to one last piece of information on this journey of ours.”

The travelers reluctantly halted their efforts and gathered around Veren. Luopon found herself standing next to him with Sula on his other side.

“Yes. You see, none of you will be able to open this Gate. Neither will I. Only your dear leader, Compass Luopon, can hope to do so.”

Luopon felt a pit in her stomach as Veren turned towards her.

“Miss Luopon, you are very special indeed. Not only do you have that peculiar life-sense that makes you a Compass, you are also a Key. Our cities must be kept shut off from the Waste, and only a few individuals in a generation are permitted such abilities. You are one of them. Simply approach the Gate, place your hands upon the metal bandings, and everyone within a dozen paces or so will be brought through the Gate and into Tarrasetan.”

Luopon grabbed the wagon as she felt her breath quicken.

“But, my memories. I’ll never make it back to Oprindell. If I go in, I’ll lose all of this.”

“Ah, Miss Luopon, that is where you are wrong. As a Compass-Key, you are immune to such afflictions.”

That didn’t make any sense. She had no memories when she appeared in the Waste all that time ago. Why should this be any different?

“I wasn’t immune to such afflictions when we started this journey. How could you possibly know that the same won’t happen this time?”

“It was a malfunction, or rather, a manifestation of the unique properties of the city from whence you came. It will not happen here. Not at the center of what civilization we have left. You have my word.”

Luopon stared at the man, trying to parse what was truth and what was unscrupulous decor.

“He’s lying.”

The words came out quickly, like Sula could keep them in no longer.

Sula? The stone beneath Luopon swayed like a ship at sea.

“We’re partners, Luo, and we’ve been lying to you.”

The world spun for a moment as Luopon’s breath escaped her. The travelers were very still. Veren’s eyes darted between Sula, Luopon, and the crowd gathered before them.

“You… what?” was all Luopon managed to get out.

“Lying. The whole time. And long before this journey. Not about everything. You really are some kind of Compass-Key combination, and that really does make you special. But you won’t keep your memories when you go through that door. You never have, not in almost a hundred journeys.”

A hundred journeys. That was… almost four years. Four years in this dead, desolate Waste, and nothing else. Not a father’s warmth or a lover’s kiss. Just four years of rock and chalk and chasms and death.

Veren\'s face had been shifting between terror and rage, but he composed himself now.

“Now, Miss Luopon, you must understand we had good reason for such nasty business.”

Luopon cleared the distance between herself and Veren in two great strides. He stumbled back, tripping over his own feet and crashing into the wagon behind him.

“Luo! Wait.”

Luopon paused.

“Just”, the older woman maneuvered herself between Luopon and the fallen man. “Just let me explain.”

Luopon didn’t speak, but she made no further moves.

“Like I said, you’re something special, and decisions were made to make the most of it. Whenever you leave a town, I’m there to guide you, just like the beginning of this trip. Encourage you, help you manage people, that’s my job. Old Veren is here in case you feel that pull towards Oprindell. He’s got a gift of his own, you see. Knows things about people, just by looking at them. This is what we do. Ten years. That’s what we signed up for. Ten years to move as many people as we could, and with you leading us the whole time.”

“You can’t leave the Waste.”

It wasn’t a question.

“That’s right.”

“Did I volunteer for this? Ten years out here?”

“No. You didn’t.”

“You wouldn’t,” snapped Veren

Luopon turned back to Veren. He was breathing heavily, leaning against the front of the wagon.

“You wanted to go to your family.” He said the word with venom. “Like Sula didn’t. Like I didn’t! Somebody had to make the decision to actually help people! I’m sorry it had to be like this. I didn’t prefer it, but I would do it a thousand times more if it meant getting more people through this Waste.”

“You know things about people? Tell me, Veren, is my family still there? In Oprindell?”

“It doesn’t matter,” Veren spat back.

“Veren!” bellowed Sula. “Tell her.”

Veren cowered tighter against the wagon, his eyes darting frantically back and forth all the while.

“Your mother and brother are in Oprindell. They’ve assumed you dead for years.”

Luopon felt like she had been punched in the gut. It was all she could do to stay upright. All she could do not to vomit on the stone. A mother and a brother. She dared not ask about her father or the existence of any other siblings. Four years she had been gone from them. Now, here at the Gate, she could make the decision to be with them again.

It would mean sentencing the rest of her party to a life not unlike her own. Maybe worse than her own, by certain measures. Stuck in a city where they didn’t belong, knowing that their families are out there while being unable to reach them. But four years. Didn’t she deserve respite? How many people had she successfully reunited with their families? She thought of Sula’s words regarding Yelo’s death. You’ve never lost one before. Luopon felt a shiver run down her spine. She wondered how many other subtleties had gone over her head these past two weeks.

The travelers stood remarkably silent. They seemed afraid. Luopon would be too, she supposed.

“I’ll wait for you, Luo.”

Luopon’s second-in-command eyed her mournfully.

“When you come back, I’ll tell you everything. Remind you of who you are. Remind you of home.”

“Sula, I-”

“You are children!” Veren spat on the ground. “The lot of you. Selfish children.”

Kara emerged from the gathered travelers and kicked at the old man’s shins. Veren dropped to the stone with a snarling grunt.

“Not just children.” He pointed up at Luopon. “You, Compass, you’re an orphan. No parents. No siblings. Nobody is waiting for you.”

“But you said I had family in Oprindell. Even if they do think I was lost to the Waste.”

“I was lying!” said Veren with wicked glee.

Luopon looked back to Sula.

“Sorry, Luo. No idea.”

Luopon considered. Her mind felt like a cup of tea stirred so violently it threatened to spill over the sides of the glass and onto the pale stone. Veren could have been lying. As painful as the idea was, she thought it unlikely. It made little sense to lie in that way if Veren’s goal was Luopon’s compliance. He was simply lashing out, saying anything he could to hurt those who had wronged him.

The more troubling possibility was that Sula had no intention of keeping her promise. This could all be another ploy, a last resort for the times Luopon was too close to ruining their plans. Luopon knew this didn’t make much sense either. Why would Sula tell her that Veren was lying about the memory loss? Even if she was somehow lying, there was nothing to be done. Sula was Luopon’s best chance. She had lied to her for four years, but Luopon suspected she had also been her best friend for that time, even if they were forced to start over every few weeks.

She looked over the crowd of travelers once more. Sula and Kara at the front, Veren behind them, still on the ground. The rest continued to look expectantly at Luopon, though she now noticed more than one glare directed towards Veren. What to do about Veren? She couldn’t kill him. She didn’t have it in her. She couldn’t let the others harm him. That somehow seemed worse.

“Fine,” Luopon said. “I’ll take you all through. Sula, wait for me. Be patient with me. I understand this will all have to be explained to me again, but I will listen to you eventually.”

“And the old man?” asked Sula.

“Why don’t you bring him in?” Kara pointed at the immense Gate.

Luopon and Sula stared blankly at the woman for a moment.

“Bring him in,” she repeated. “It keeps him away from Sula. It takes him out of the Waste, where could get up to more trouble. It might even jog your memory once you’re inside. Luopon from the inside has met Veren, right? She might know to get away from him. You might, I mean.”

Veren’s face had flattened into a mask of horror. He had lived four years straight in the Waste. If he passed through those doors, the version of himself he had been for nearly half a decade would be lost. A fitting punishment.

Luopon looked to Veren once more and then back to Kara. The Compass nodded once.

“Restrain him and then line up. We’re going through.”

* * *

Luopon stood at the base of the Gate. Before her were two handles of black metal. She could have stacked her own hands a dozen times over on the vertical bars. They were joined to the pale wood of the Gate by large nails with heads as big as Luopon’s palm. She exhaled, meaning to slow her heartbeat, and stole a glance backwards towards Sula. The woman nodded once.

Steeling herself, Luopon reached out and grasped the Gate’s handles with both hands. They were cold, colder than anything she had felt in weeks. Then, without warning, the sensation vanished. Everything vanished. The door melted into white light. Still gripping something, though to her eyes it seemed her hands were wrapped around open air, Luopon craned her neck to look behind her. Her surroundings had warped into pure blinding light. She had to trust that her party was still close. The light enveloped her and somehow grew brighter still. Her thoughts felt distant, as though she were fighting sleep. Her view of her hands blurred until she could no longer see them. The stone beneath her disappeared, but she felt no sensation of falling. It seemed, she thought hazily, that she could feel nothing at all.

* * *

Luopon awoke, dazed but intact. She was lying on a hard surface. She propped herself up on two arms and tried to take a look around. The world spun slightly. She could make out that she was at the bottom of a rounded set of wide stairs emanating up in large circle. She saw people, maybe thirty of them, descending the stairs towards her. Behind her lay another dozen people. They were covered in chalk and many were bright red, like they had spent hours under a blazing sun. A young girl with orange hair helped an even younger child to his feet. A woman near Luopon’s age moved frantically as she seemed to search the weary travelers’ faces.

Her eyes settled on an old man. Wrinkled and confused as he was, his visage reminded her of a newborn. He blinked slowly into the light of day, scanning the approaching figures. The people were nearly upon them now. Some ran towards the travelers in pairs and trios. Others approached cautiously, as if afraid to look too closely at the party. One man in particular, all beard and bushy eyebrows, seemed pleased at first, but his demeanor shifted upon noticing the old man.

Luopon watched the bearded man’s eyes bulge as they came to rest on the old man. He turned and shouted something behind him. Luopon couldn’t tell what; her senses were still… unbalanced. She watched him run and clutch the other man. Maybe his brother? But, this was not a hug. The man shouted again and again and shook the elder as though trying to wake him from a nightmare. The yells came muffled to Luopon’s ears. Between shakes, the bearded man scanned the unwashed arrivals.

Luopon’s memories began to mend themselves together. The white chalk. The travelers. She was a Compass. In the Waste, she was the only one who could lead. She had set out for Oprindell and… Luopon turned her eyes to the city beyond. Spires accented the skyline between densely packed buildings of dark wood and stone. This wasn’t Oprindell at all. Hadn’t she already made it to Oprindell? What was she doing here?

Another glance at the old man, now coming to his senses, and the memories came flooding violently into her mind. A family reunited. That same family, torn apart too soon. Torn apart by that man.

Luopon shot to her feet, suddenly rejuvenated by shock and rage. She strode towards the old man, Veren, she now remembered. Before she closed the distance, the bearded man locked eyes with her and scowled.

“She is here!” he shouted.

Three figures emerged from the onlookers and hurried towards Luopon. Luopon reoriented herself and dashed in the opposite direction. She dodged travelers and their loved ones, weaving through the crowd. She looked over her shoulder and saw her pursuers close behind. Though energized, Luopon’s body was still sluggish, and she was slowed down by each person she jostled or tripped over.

She felt a hand grip her shoulder, but she jerked away and dove between the closing ranks of a family gathering into an embrace. On the other side, she was met with the Gate. Twenty meters of white wood adorned with black metal; handles impractically large. She turned back, gasping, and looked upon her pursuers.

“Compass Luopon,” said the bearded man. “I’m sorry for the scare. Just come with us. We will get you a bath and a hot meal and we can talk about things.”

He had his hands out as though he were calming a wild animal.

Luopon reached back with one hand and placed it on the Gate handle. A surge of power rushed through Luopon, shaking the Gate and the ground beneath her. The three men stumbled backwards, but the bearded one stepped closer.

“Compass Luopon, please. You remember what happens if you open that Gate, don’t you?”

“I remember just fine.”

She turned her back to the city and placed both hands on the Gate. With a blinding flash of white light, Luopon, Compass, disappeared once more.
